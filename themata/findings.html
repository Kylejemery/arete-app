The Themata Project · Phase 5 draft

Chrysippus's themata, machine checked

Which of Chrysippus's reduction rules, the θέματα, under which readings of the Stoic connectives, derive exactly the arguments the ancient sources accept and none of the ones they reject?

Not for publication Draft for specialist review. None of the 39 ledger entries has been signed off yet, so every result here is provisional.
The question

Chrysippus held that every syllogistic argument is one of five indemonstrables, or reduces to them by four meta rules. Some of those rules survive in the sources. The rest are modern reconstructions.

The brief allowed three outcomes: one reconstruction fits uniquely, several fit, or none fit. Every candidate was run against a ledger of argument forms quoted from verified sources, and each verdict was checked in Lean 4.

The answer

Several fit, so the evidence underdetermines the themata. It still fixes three features of the logic.

The first thema is required

Origen reports the "two conditionals" argument as a Stoic form, with the Stoics' own example: if you know that you are dead, you are dead; if you know that you are dead, you are not dead; therefore you do not know that you are dead. No candidate without a first thema derives it, and that is proved. So the attested third thema alone does not fit.

Contraction is needed, and weakening never happens

Every fit lets a repeated premise count once, through the set view or the merging cut. No candidate under any setting derives the redundant argument (S016). A relevance semantics, where contraction holds and weakening fails, proves it.

The contradictory must cancel a negation

Every fit reads the contradictory of "not p" as p. Read it as "not not p" and the Sorites chain (S018) is provably underivable, in a Gödel logic where double negation does not collapse.

The new witness

"The concrete example which the Stoics give to illustrate this form of argument is the following: 1st, If you know that you are dead, you are dead; 2d, if you know that you are dead, you are not dead. And the conclusion is, 'you do not know that you are dead.'"

Origen, Against Celsus VII.15, translated by Frederick Crombie, Ante-Nicene Fathers IV (1885). Origen calls the form "the theorem of two propositions". The general schema sentence rests on an editorial emendation (the translators' note 4701); the Stoic example does not.

How "underivable" is established

Searching and not finding proves nothing. A failure counts as proven only when a Lean theorem covers it, and none of these uses sorry.

The relevance semantics and the closed valuations are proof devices. They are not claims about the logic the Stoics held.

Results in detail
  • No overgeneration anywhere. No candidate under any setting derives what the ledger rejects, and every such cell is proven.
  • Fits by view. 100 fits use the set view. The other 58 use the merging cut, 50 under multiset and 8 under list. All 158 use the toggle contradictory.
  • Sixteen candidates never fit. Seven lack a third thema, which the composite arguments need. Nine lack a first thema, which S020 needs.
  • Redundancy. S016 is valid by the Stoic validity criterion (Diogenes Laertius 7.77) under every reading of the conditional, so its rejection rests on redundancy alone. S009 and S011 are redundant under the strict reading and not under the narrow one. The ledger is consistent only if indifferently concluding arguments were exempt.
  • Asserted falsity is not negation. S008, "'p and q' is false; p; therefore not q", which Diogenes calls conclusive but not syllogistic, is proven underivable by every candidate, as the ledger requires.
Open and underdetermined

What the evidence cannot yet decide

  • The shape of the first thema, which premise it contraposes, and the shape of the cut: every variant fits somewhere.
  • A separate second or fourth thema: no attested argument in the ledger needs one beyond contraposition and cut.
  • Chrysippus against Antipater on single premise arguments, by how those items were ruled.

The pointed question

  • A summary of Bobzien's reconstruction reports it as monotonic and without contraction. Every fit here needs contraction and none has weakening. Whether that conflict is real waits on her paper, which is not in the corpus.
Limits
  • The ledger is small, 18 formal items, and unsigned. Sextus Against the Logicians, Galen and Alexander, the main ancient sources for the themata, are not yet available.
  • S020 rests on Origen, a later witness who is not a Stoic. Its quotation is verified against the stored text.
  • Mates is represented by a summary, not his text. Reading the dialectical theorem as a merging cut is our own interpretation.
  • The Sorites runs as a three link chain. Under the list view, results depend on the order in which the ledger lists premises.
  • Derivations shown by the harness are evaluations of the same rules the proofs are about, not kernel checked certificates, except two proved in Lean.
  • The search runs to depth 8 over each item's own formulas. Only the 54 unproven non-fits depend on these bounds.
  • The reading of Alexander on the synthetic theorem (T04) is our own reading of untranslated Greek and is pending review.
For the reviewer

Seven questions

  1. Are the ledger verdicts right, especially S011 (valid), S016 (invalid, reported only through Mates), S008 (conclusive but not syllogistic), the Sorites form taken as valid, and S020 from Origen?
  2. Is Origen's report of "the theorem of two propositions" good evidence that the Stoics treated the form as reducible by the themata, and does the emendation in note 4701 affect that?
  3. Every fit needs a repeated premise to count once. Is that consistent with Stoic analysis, and does it conflict with Bobzien's reconstruction?
  4. Is reading the contradictory of "not p" as p the standard view, given Diogenes Laertius 7.69 on double negation?
  5. Is our encoding of Simplicius's third thema, a cut with the conclusion first and one further premise, fair to the Greek?
  6. Is reading the dialectical theorem as a merging cut defensible?
  7. Did Alexander say the Stoics made their second, third and fourth themata from the synthetic theorem?
