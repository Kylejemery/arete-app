## **Course Philosophy**

Most people who read Marcus Aurelius, Epictetus, or Chrysippus are reading someone else's interpretation of the text. Translation is inherently interpretive — every choice of English word encodes a philosophical assumption. GREK 101 gives you the tools to go upstream.

This is not a classical philology course. It is Greek for philosophers — focused on the vocabulary, grammar, and syntactic patterns that recur in Stoic primary texts. By Session 15, students will be able to read simple passages from Epictetus's Encheiridion with a lexicon. By Session 30, students will read short passages from the Discourses unaided.

### **Part 1 — Why Greek?**

Before we learn a single letter, consider what is at stake. When you read 'virtue' in a translation of Marcus Aurelius, you are reading the translator's best approximation of the Greek aretē (ἀρετή). But aretē does not mean exactly what 'virtue' means in English. It carries connotations of excellence, of a thing functioning at its peak — the aretē of a knife is sharpness, the aretē of an eye is clear vision. The aretē of a human being, for the Stoics, is the full exercise of reason.

'Virtue' in English has overtones of moral purity, even puritanism. Aretē does not. The distance between the two words is the distance between what you have been reading and what Epictetus actually said. This course closes that distance.

### **Part 3 — Breathing Marks**

Every Greek word beginning with a vowel carries a breathing mark — either smooth (') or rough (h sound). These appear above the first vowel.

- Smooth breathing (᾿) — no h sound. Example: ἀρετή (aretē), not haretē

- Rough breathing (῾) — adds an h sound. Example: ἡγεμονικόν (hēgemonikon) — note the h

- Rho (ρ) at the start of a word always takes rough breathing: ῥήτωρ (rhētōr)

- When a diphthong begins a word, the breathing mark sits over the second vowel: οἰκείωσις (oikeiōsis)

## **Course Philosophy**

Marcus Aurelius wrote in Greek. But Seneca wrote in Latin — and Seneca is arguably the most quotable philosopher in the Western tradition. More importantly, it was Latin that carried Stoic ideas into the medieval period, the Renaissance, and ultimately into the modern world. Every educated person in Europe from 500 AD to 1800 read Seneca, Cicero, and Marcus in Latin. The philosophical vocabulary of the West — virtus, ratio, natura, providentia — is Roman.

LATN 101 focuses on Seneca's prose style: periodic sentences, the second person address, the moral aphorism. Seneca is the most accessible Latin writer for a non-specialist, and his Letters to Lucilius are among the greatest documents in the history of philosophy. The course targets the ability to read Epistulae Morales I.1 — the opening letter — by Session 15.

| **Course Target** Primary text target: Seneca, Epistulae Morales I.1 — 'Ita fac, mi Lucili: vindica te tibi.' — 'Do this, my Lucilius: claim yourself for yourself.' The entire course is structured around the ability to read and translate this letter by Session 15. |

### **Part 1 — Why Seneca?**

'Ita fac, mi Lucili: vindica te tibi.' Do this, my Lucilius: claim yourself for yourself. Seneca wrote this line roughly 65 AD, weeks or months before Nero forced him to commit suicide. It is one of the most concentrated sentences in philosophical history — four words that contain an entire theory of life.

Vindica te tibi. The verb vindicare means to claim, to defend, to liberate. The object is te — yourself. And tibi — for yourself, to yourself. The sentence is in the second person, addressed directly to you. Seneca writes the entire Letters to Lucilius in this mode: the philosopher speaking to the specific person in front of him, demanding something.

By Session 15 of this course, you will be able to read and parse this sentence — and the letter it comes from — without a translation. That is the goal. Everything before Session 15 is in service of that moment.

### **Part 2 — The Latin Alphabet**

The Latin alphabet is the ancestor of our own. With two exceptions (no J or W in classical Latin; V served double duty as vowel and consonant), it is the same 23 letters. The key difference is pronunciation — classical Latin sounds quite different from the church Latin many people first encounter.

| **Letter** | **Classical Sound** | **Note** | **Example** |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| A | a as in father | Always open — never as in 'cat' | animus (soul, mind) |
| B | b | Standard | bonus (good) |
| C | k always | Never soft c — Caesar = KAI-sar | Cicero = KI-ke-ro |
| D | d | Standard | dico (I say) |
| E | e as in pet (short) | Long ē as in 'they' | esse (to be) |
| F | f | Standard | facio (I do/make) |
| G | g hard always | Never soft — regina = re-GI-na | genus (kind/race) |
| H | h (light aspirate) | Barely voiced, sometimes silent | homo (human being) |
| I | i as in machine | Long ī; as consonant = y in 'yes' | ira (anger) |
| K | k | Rare — mostly in Kalends | (rare) |
| L | l (clear, never dark) | Always clear — never as in 'full' | liber (free/book) |
| M | m | Standard; final m often light | mens (mind) |
| N | n | Standard | natura (nature) |
| O | o as in more (short) | Long ō as in 'tone' | otium (leisure) |
| P | p | Aspirated slightly | pax (peace) |
| Q | qu = kw | Always followed by u | qui (who) |
| R | r (trilled) | Always trilled — no English r | ratio (reason) |
| S | s always | Never z sound — rosa = RO-sa | sapientia (wisdom) |
| T | t always | Never sh — ratio = RA-ti-o | tempus (time) |
| V | w or u | As consonant: w. As vowel: u. | virtus = WIR-tus |
| X | ks | Standard | exemplum (example) |
| Y | ü (French u) | Only in Greek loanwords | tyrannus (tyrant) |
| Z | z (or dz) | Only in Greek loanwords | zona (zone) |

## **Course Philosophy**

The Stoics built the first complete propositional logic system in the history of philosophy — 300 years before Frege. Chrysippus of Soli (c. 279–206 BC), the third head of the Stoic school, was widely regarded in antiquity as the greatest logician who ever lived. It was said that 'if there were a logic among the gods, it would be that of Chrysippus.'

This history is almost entirely invisible to modern readers of Stoicism. Popularizations of Stoic philosophy focus on the ethics — on Marcus Aurelius's aphorisms about equanimity, on Epictetus's dichotomy of control. These are genuine and important. But they sit atop a complete logical and epistemological system that the Stoics developed with extraordinary precision.

PHIL 705 recovers that system. You will learn how the Stoics analyzed language, truth, and inference — and how that logical architecture connects to their ethics. The dichotomy of control is not a self-help insight. It is a consequence of a theory of assent, which is a consequence of a theory of impressions, which sits inside a theory of knowledge. This course shows the architecture.

| **Pedagogical Note** The Stoics distinguished three parts of philosophy: logic, physics, and ethics — in that order. They began with logic because they believed you could not think clearly about anything else until you understood how thought itself worked. PHIL 705 follows the same sequence. |
| --- |

### **What is Logic? The Stoic Answer**

| **Pre-Seminar Briefing — Session 1** Pre-Seminar Briefing  THE PROBLEM: Most people who study Stoic philosophy encounter it as a set of practices or attitudes — journaling, negative visualization, the dichotomy of control. This is not wrong, but it is incomplete. The practices rest on a theory. The theory rests on a logic. This course begins at the foundation.  WHY IT MATTERS: The Stoics divided philosophy into three parts: logic, physics, ethics. They began with logic — not because it was most interesting, but because they believed clear thinking was the precondition for clear living. If you cannot analyze how impressions work, you cannot understand why assent matters. If you do not understand assent, the dichotomy of control is just a slogan.  WHAT TO WATCH FOR: In this session, notice how the Stoics define logos. The same word means both rational speech and reason itself. This is not an accident — for the Stoics, language and thought are not two different things. Logic is the study of logos in both senses.  YOUR TASK: Come prepared to defend or challenge this claim: 'Philosophy must begin with logic, not ethics.' |

### **Part 1 — The Stoic Division of Philosophy**

Diogenes Laertius reports that the Stoics divided philosophy into three parts: logic (logikē), physics (physikē), and ethics (ēthikē). This division was not merely organizational — it was a claim about the order in which philosophical questions must be addressed.

Various analogies were used to explain the relationship. Some Stoics compared philosophy to an egg: logic is the shell, physics is the white, ethics is the yolk — the thing protected and nourished by all the rest. Others compared it to a fertile field: logic is the fence, physics is the soil, ethics is the crop.

Both analogies make the same point: you cannot access what matters most — how to live — without first securing the perimeter (clear thinking) and preparing the ground (understanding the natural world). Ethics without logic is inspiration without structure. It may move you, but it cannot guide you.

| *'**They say that philosophical doctrine has three parts: one concerned with physics, one with ethics, and one with logic. Zeno of Citium first made this division.**'** — Diogenes Laertius VII.39* |
| --- |

### **Part 2 — What is Logic For the Stoics?**

The Stoics used the word 'logic' (logikē) more broadly than modern usage. For them, logic included:

- Formal logic — the analysis of valid argument forms (what we would recognize as logic today)

- Rhetoric — the study of persuasion and extended discourse

- Grammar — the analysis of language and its parts

- Epistemology — the theory of knowledge, impressions, and the criterion of truth

All of these fall under logic because all of them concern logos — rational speech and reason. The Stoics did not sharply separate the study of how we think from the study of how we speak. For them, thought is a kind of inner speech, and speech is externalized thought. Logic studies the whole of this.

| **KEY CONCEPT: LOGOS** Logos: the single most important word in the Stoic philosophical vocabulary. It means: (1) rational speech — the ability to use language governed by reason; (2) reason itself — the faculty that distinguishes humans from animals; (3) the rational principle pervading the cosmos — the Stoic God. Logic studies logos in senses (1) and (2). Physics and theology deal with sense (3). But they are the same word. |
| --- |

### **Part 3 — Why Logic Comes First**

A critic might object: surely ethics is the most important part of philosophy? Shouldn't we begin with the question of how to live, rather than abstract questions about valid argument forms?

The Stoic answer is that you cannot reliably answer ethical questions without first being able to distinguish good from bad reasoning. The person who approaches ethics without logic is at the mercy of whoever makes the most compelling-sounding argument. The sophisticated Stoic can evaluate the argument itself — not just whether it feels persuasive.

More specifically: the Stoic ethical theory depends on a theory of assent (synkatathesis). The central claim — that only virtue is good, that externals are indifferent — is not obvious. Reaching it and holding it requires the ability to resist bad impressions and withhold assent from them. And that ability requires understanding what an impression is and how assent works. That understanding is logic.
