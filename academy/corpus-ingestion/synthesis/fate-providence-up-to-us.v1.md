# Fate, Providence, and What Is Up to Us: A Summary

The Stoics held that everything happens by fate, and also that people are responsible for what they do. Critics from Carneades onward said those two claims cannot both be true. Chrysippus spent much of his career arguing that they can, and Tim O'Keefe calls the Stoics the first clearly compatibilist thinkers in the Western tradition.

This summary draws on the Arete corpus: Cicero's _On Fate_ and _On the Nature of the Gods_, Epictetus's _Discourses_, Seneca's _On Providence_ and _Letters_, Marcus Aurelius, Plutarch's attacks on the Stoics, Pearson's collection of Zeno and Cleanthes, and the scholarship of Arnold, Hicks, Zeller, O'Keefe, Bobzien, Vázquez, and Šuster. It is a companion to _Stoic Logic: A Summary_, which covers the logical side of this debate in more detail.

## The problem in brief

Three Stoic commitments pull against each other:

1. **Fate.** Every event, including every human choice, follows from prior causes in an unbroken chain.

2. **Providence.** That chain is the work of a good and rational god, arranging things for the best.

3. **Responsibility.** People deserve praise and blame for what they do, and some things are genuinely up to them.

If fate is true, critics asked, why deliberate, why call a doctor, and why blame anyone? If providence is true, why do good people suffer? The Stoic answers to those questions are the subject of what follows.

## Fate: the chain of causes

For the Stoics, fate is not a blind force but the rational order of the world seen as a sequence of causes. Cicero gives the standard definition: fate is the order and series of causes, each linked to the next and producing its effect (_On Divination_, via Arnold).

Chrysippus, Posidonius, and Zeno all held that everything happens by fate (Arnold). Seneca called it the necessity of all things and actions, which no force can break (Arnold).

The Stoics gave it several names, which is itself revealing. Zeno called fate a power that moves matter by fixed laws, and said it could equally be called providence or nature. Chrysippus called it a spiritual force ordering the universe, the reason (_logos_) of the cosmos, and the law by which past things have happened, present things happen, and future things will happen (Arnold).

The doctrine rests on Stoic physics. The cosmos is a single living body pervaded by a divine fire or breath, and only bodies can act or be acted on. There is no motion without a cause, so each effect becomes a cause in turn. Arnold draws the conclusion the Stoics drew: whoever knew every cause could trace every consequence.

Chrysippus also argued for fate from logic. Every proposition is either true or false. A motion with no cause would make some propositions about it neither true nor false. So every motion has a cause, and everything happens by fate (Cicero, _On Fate_). This is why Chrysippus fought so hard to prove that every proposition is true or false, and why Epicurus, to escape fate, was willing to deny it.

## Providence and god

The Stoic god is not a craftsman standing outside the world. O'Keefe explains that god is the cosmos itself, a designing fire whose reason runs through everything, and fate is how that providence is carried out. So the Stoic chain of causes is also, from another angle, the plan of a good mind.

Chrysippus and Cleanthes disagreed on how far the two coincide. Both held that whatever happens by providence also happens by fate. Chrysippus went further and said whatever happens by fate happens by providence; Cleanthes would not, because he did not want providence responsible for evil (Arnold, citing Chalcidius).

Providence, on the Stoic account, made the world to last, made it complete, and made it beautiful. Cicero's Stoic spokesman says it is the best world that could have been made from the materials available, and that it exists for the sake of rational beings, gods and humans (_On the Nature of the Gods_, via Arnold). Chrysippus pressed this into details that his critics loved to mock: horses help us fight, the peacock exists for its tail, and the flea is useful for waking us up (Arnold). Seneca was more modest, warning that humans flatter themselves if they think the whole universe was built for them (Arnold).

## The problem of evil

If a good god orders everything, why do earthquakes flatten cities, hail ruin careful farmers, and Athens put Socrates to death (Arnold)? The Stoics gave several answers, and Arnold notes that the sheer number of them betrays how hard the question was.

**Logical answers.** Chrysippus argued that good implies its opposite: there could be no justice without injustice, no courage without cowardice, no truth without falsehood. He compared vice to the coarse lines in a comedy, bad on their own but part of the play's charm (Arnold, citing Gellius and Plutarch).

**Side effects.** Some evils follow as unavoidable byproducts of good design. The design of the human head required thin, delicate bones, and the price is that it is easily injured (Arnold, citing Gellius). Other evils may have purposes we have not yet discovered, just as we do not yet know what every animal is for.

**Moral answers.** Seneca's _On Providence_ makes the case most memorably. Lucilius asked why, if providence rules, so many evils befall good men. Seneca answers that no evil can befall a good man, because good and evil cannot mix; misfortune tests him, and it matters less what you bear than how you bear it. God treats good people like a strict father, not an indulgent mother: he trains them with hardship so they gain real strength. Epictetus uses the image of a wrestling trainer who matches you with a tough opponent (Arnold).

**Limits on god.** In the end the Stoics conceded that providence works within necessity. Chrysippus admitted that there is much necessity mixed into things, and explained failed prophecies by saying god cannot know everything (Arnold, citing Plutarch). Arnold reads this as a retreat to Cleanthes's more cautious position. It also produced one of the more moving Stoic ideas: if god gave us what he could rather than what he would, then humans are not tools of providence but its fellow workers (Arnold, citing Seneca).

## Truth, possibility, and fate

The ancient debate over fate was fought as much in logic as in physics, and Cicero's _On Fate_ is the main surviving record. Three rival positions shaped it.

|Thinker|Position|Consequence|
|---|---|---|
|Diodorus|Only what is or will be true is possible|Whatever will not happen is impossible; the future is as fixed as the past|
|Chrysippus|Every proposition is true or false, and every event has a cause, but some things are possible that never happen|Fate without necessity: the breakable gem that is never broken, the oracle about Cypselus that could have failed|
|Epicurus|To escape fate, deny that every proposition about the future is true or false, and let atoms swerve without a cause|Freedom bought with uncaused motion, which Cicero calls absurd|

Cicero presses Chrysippus with an astrologer's rule: if anyone is born at the rising of the dog star, he will not die at sea. If that conditional is true and the birth is past, and past truths are necessary, then Fabius's not dying at sea looks necessary too, which is Diodorus's view. Chrysippus's escape was to have the astrologers restate their rules as negated conjunctions: it is not the case that someone is born under the dog star and dies at sea. Cicero found the suggestion ridiculous and asked whether doctors and geometers must rephrase their knowledge the same way (_On Fate_).

Carneades cut deeper. He argued that the Epicureans did not need the swerve at all: the soul's voluntary motion is caused by its own nature, not by anything outside, so it is caused without being fated. On his account, even Apollo could not foresee what has no cause already present in nature (_On Fate_). Cicero himself, in an Academic mood, said he would sooner deny that every proposition is true or false than grant that fate governs everything. The logical side of this dispute, including Diodorus's Master Argument, is covered in _Stoic Logic: A Summary_.

## The Lazy Argument and co-fated events

The most practical objection to fate was the "lazy argument" (_argos logos_). Cicero states it this way: if it is fated that you recover from this illness, you will recover whether or not you call a doctor; if it is fated that you do not, you will not recover either way; so calling a doctor is pointless (_On Fate_). Taken seriously, it would justify doing nothing at all.

Chrysippus answered with the idea of "co-fated" events (_confatalia_). Some fated facts are simple, like "Socrates will die on such a day," which will happen whatever he does. Others are joined: "Laius will beget Oedipus" cannot be fated apart from Laius lying with his wife, and "Milo will wrestle at Olympia" cannot be fated apart from an opponent. Recovery and calling the doctor are fated together (_On Fate_). Arnold adds an example from Homer: soldiers told to keep watch cannot reason that the ships are safe if fate protects them, because there is no such thing as a watch kept by sleepers or a victory won by those who run.

O'Keefe notes the limit of this reply. It shows that our actions matter as causes, but it does not yet show that we are responsible for them. That needed a further argument.

## Causes and the cylinder

Chrysippus's central move was to distinguish kinds of cause. Cicero reports that he separated "absolute principal" causes from "auxiliary proximate" ones. When the Stoics say everything happens by prior causes, they mean the second kind: causes that start something off without fully determining what it becomes (_On Fate_).

His illustration is the cylinder. Push a cylinder and you give it the start of its motion, but not its rolling; it rolls because it is a cylinder. The example is usually paired with a cone, which pushed the same way moves differently because of its own shape (Vázquez; Šuster). In the same way, an impression strikes the mind from outside, but how the mind responds, whether it assents, depends on the mind's own nature (_On Fate_).

Scholars in the corpus read the analogy in similar terms. O'Keefe: impressions trigger action, but how one responds is a function of the person one is. Vázquez: the external impression is not enough to settle the character of the effect, because assent lies with the agent's commanding faculty. Šuster classes the cylinder as an explanatory analogy rather than a proof, showing the priority of the agent's inner nature within the total set of causes, and defends it against Alexander of Aphrodisias's objection that it would apply just as well to animals and objects.

Cicero concluded that, properly explained, Chrysippus and his opponents differed more in words than in substance: both agreed that some effects follow from causes we cannot prevent, while others, though caused, remain ours to shape. Cicero also judged that Chrysippus, while trying to take a middle path, slid back toward necessity by his own language (_On Fate_).

## What is up to us

The phrase _eph' hēmin_, "up to us," names what the Stoics saved from fate. It is not the ability to have done otherwise, but the fact that assent comes from us. O'Keefe explains that in rational animals every real action requires assent to an impression about how to act, and because assent flows from the agent's own reason and character rather than outside force, the action is truly the agent's.

Epictetus made this the center of his teaching. Education, he says, means learning to apply our general notions of good and bad to particular cases, and learning to distinguish what is in our power from what is not. In our power are the will and everything that depends on it; not in our power are the body, possessions, parents, children, country, and everyone we live among (_Discourses_ I.22). O'Keefe notes Epictetus's claim that the correct use of impressions is the one thing god placed entirely under our control.

Freedom follows from this. The free person is the one who cannot be restrained, and the only way never to be restrained is to desire nothing that belongs to others, meaning nothing outside our own will (_Discourses_ IV.1).

## Responsibility without alternatives

The Stoics did not think responsibility requires that you could have done otherwise. O'Keefe calls this their rejection of the principle of alternate possibilities, and reconstructs two arguments for it.

**The ethical argument.** The virtuous person cannot act viciously; virtue is a reliable skill that rules it out. If praise required the ability to do otherwise, the best people would be the least praiseworthy, which is absurd. Seneca says the good man cannot fail to do right, not because he is forced but because he cannot want otherwise (O'Keefe).

**The metaphysical argument.** If a person in exactly the same inner and outer circumstances could act differently on different occasions, the difference would have no cause. That would make action random, not free. On this view, Carneades's self-moving will only moves the gap instead of closing it (O'Keefe).

## Following willingly

The practical side of Stoic fate is less an argument than an attitude: accept what happens as part of a rational whole, and make your will agree with it. Pearson calls this the true Stoic answer to the charge that providence rules out freedom.

**Cleanthes's prayer.** The best known expression is a short poem by Cleanthes, preserved by Epictetus (_Handbook_ 53). In G. H. Rendall's translation, quoted by Arnold:

Lead me, O Zeus, and lead me, Destiny, What way soe'er ye have appointed me! I follow unafraid: yea, though the will Turn recreant, I needs must follow still.

Seneca translated the lines into Latin and added one of his own that became a proverb: the fates lead the willing and drag the unwilling (_Letters_ 107; Pearson; Hicks).

**The dog and the cart.** The same idea appears as an image reported by Hippolytus. A dog tied to a cart follows willingly if it chooses, and is dragged if it does not; either way it goes where the cart goes. So with people: those who will not follow are compelled all the same (Zeller). The attribution is disputed. Vázquez notes it is credited to Zeno and may mean that only large outward events are fated, not inner attitudes; Šuster reports that Bobzien doubts it is Chrysippean at all; and O'Keefe warns against reading it as fatalism, since it would make god override human resistance, which does not fit Chrysippus's view.

**Epictetus.** Epictetus returns to Cleanthes's lines again and again. Keep the words ready, he tells a student: if it is god's will that I go to Rome, I go to Rome; to the island of Gyara, to Gyara; to prison, to prison (_Discourses_ IV.4). He treats logic and eloquence as inns on the road home, pleasant places to pass through, while the real purpose is to use impressions rightly and be able to say those lines with your whole soul (_Discourses_ II.23).

**Marcus Aurelius.** Marcus often draws the contrast between what is up to us and what is not. Pearson points to his remark that only a rational being is given the power to follow events willingly, while merely following is compulsory for everything (_Meditations_ X.28). Zeller adds Marcus's thought that even the person who complains and resists still does work the universe needed.

## Critics and verdicts

The Stoic position drew fire from every side, and much of what we know of it survives inside the attacks.

|Critic|Objection|
|---|---|
|Carneades|If everything happens by prior causes, nothing is up to us; but some things are up to us; so not everything happens by fate (Cicero, _On Fate_)|
|Cicero|Chrysippus's distinction of causes ends up affirming necessity in spite of itself; his advice to astrologers is a verbal trick|
|Epicurus|Rejected fate by making atoms swerve without cause, a move Cicero thought worse than the disease|
|Alexander of Aphrodisias|The cylinder model fits a stone as well as a person, so it cannot capture human agency (via Šuster)|
|Plutarch|Collected Chrysippus's statements on fate, god, and evil as proof that he contradicted himself (via Arnold)|
|Kant|Dismissed this kind of compatibilism as a wretched subterfuge (via Šuster)|

Modern scholarship is kinder. O'Keefe treats the Stoics as the founders of compatibilism, with a worked-out theory of action and an argument that responsibility does not need alternative possibilities. Bobzien's modal logic shows how Chrysippus's definition of possibility, cut between Philo and Diodorus, made room for unrealized possibilities and so for deliberation, while noting that possibility alone does not make something up to us. Arnold, writing in 1911, judged that the many Stoic answers on evil betrayed a weak case, but admired the ethics that grew from the concession that god works within necessity.

## Where the evidence runs thin

Chrysippus wrote at length on fate, providence, and possibility, and none of it survives whole. Our main primary source, Cicero's _On Fate_, is written by a critic and has lost both its beginning and its end. Alexander's _On Fate_ and Gellius's quotations of Chrysippus are not in the corpus and reach this summary through Arnold and Zeller. Plutarch's _On Stoic Self-Contradictions_ is in the corpus and deserves a closer reading than this summary gives it. Who first used the dog and cart, and what it meant, remains disputed. Readers should treat the reconstructions of Chrysippus's exact views here as the scholars' reconstructions, not his words.
