# The Virtues of Socrates in Plato and Xenophon

Socrates wrote nothing, so his character comes down to us through two students who knew him: Plato and Xenophon. The Stoics held him up as the nearest example of the wise man, and Epictetus held him up again and again as proof that a human being can live by virtue alone.

This list gathers the moments in their writings that show Socrates practicing the four cardinal virtues. It draws on the works in the Arete corpus: Plato's _Apology_ and _Gorgias_ (Jowett's translation) and Xenophon's _Memorabilia_ (Dakyns's translation). References to Plato use the standard page numbers, like _Apology_ 28e; references to Xenophon give book, chapter, and section, like _Memorabilia_ I.2.1. Several of the most famous episodes come from dialogues not yet in the corpus, and they are listed separately at the end. Xenophon closes his book with his own summary of the man: so pious that he did nothing without the counsel of the gods, so just that he never harmed anyone, so self-controlled that he never chose the pleasant over the better, so wise that he never misjudged better from worse, and skilled at testing others and urging them toward virtue (_Memorabilia_ IV.8.11).

## How to read this document

**Socrates is the example; the sage is the ideal; you are in training.** The Stoics held that the perfectly wise person, the sage, is so rare that perhaps none has ever lived, and they pointed to Socrates as the nearest example. Epictetus told his students that though they were not yet Socrates, they should live as ones seeking to be Socrates (_Handbook_ 51). Everything here describes what the sage would do. The reader is always the one making progress toward it, whom the Stoics called a _prokopton_.

**The verdicts are about judgments, not things.** For the Stoics, virtue is the only good and vice the only evil. Food, drink, screens, money, and the body are indifferents: some preferred, some not, none good or bad in themselves. What is good or bad is how you judge them and what you let them make of you (_Handbook_ 5). A verdict of **Avoid** does not say that a snack or a game is itself a vice. It says that giving in to it trains a habit of assenting to pleasure without choosing, and every habit grows by the acts that feed it (_Discourses_ II.18). The ancients still gave firm rules about indifferents, and so does this document.

**Three kinds of material.** The lists of what Socrates did come from Plato and Xenophon. The rules for living come from the Stoics who followed him, each cited to its text. The verdict tables on modern life are this document's own applications of those sources, not the ancients' words.

**Socrates's wisdom is the trainee's wisdom.** Socrates said he was wise only in knowing that he did not know. The Stoic sage is defined the other way: he never holds a mere opinion and never assents to anything false (Diogenes Laertius VII.121). Knowing the limits of your knowledge is where the one in training stands; the sage stands at the end of the road.

**The sage is not grim.** The Stoics held that the sage feels three good emotions: joy, the rational counterpart of pleasure; caution, the rational counterpart of fear; and wishing, the rational counterpart of desire (Diogenes Laertius VII.116). The standard here removes what disturbs you, not what delights you.

**Attention, not anxiety.** Epictetus asks for constant attention, but its aim is calm. If watching yourself turns into anxious self-surveillance, you have made a practice meant for freedom into a new master.

## Courage

- **He held his post in battle.** Socrates served as a soldier at Potidaea, Amphipolis, and Delium and stayed where his commanders stationed him, facing death. He argued he must hold his post in philosophy just as firmly, where the god had placed him (_Apology_ 28e).

- **He refused to stop philosophizing, even to save his life.** Offered release on condition that he stop questioning people, he said he would obey the god rather than the Athenians and would not change his ways even if he had to die many times (_Apology_ 29c to 30c).

- **He stood alone against the Assembly at the trial of the generals.** When the Athenians wanted to try the generals of Arginusae together, which was illegal, Socrates was the only presiding officer to oppose it, though the orators threatened to impeach and arrest him (_Apology_ 32b; _Memorabilia_ I.1.18, IV.4.2).

- **He defied the Thirty Tyrants over Leon of Salamis.** Ordered with four others to bring in Leon to be executed, Socrates simply went home, knowing it might cost him his life (_Apology_ 32c to 32d; _Memorabilia_ IV.4.3).

- **He mocked the Thirty openly.** While they were killing citizens, he remarked that a herdsman who kept shrinking his herd would be called a poor herdsman, and so should a ruler who kept shrinking the city. The remark was reported to Critias and Charicles (_Memorabilia_ I.2.32).

- **He questioned Critias and Charicles when they tried to silence him.** Summoned and forbidden to talk with young men, he calmly asked them to define their terms, until Charicles lost his temper (_Memorabilia_ I.2.33 to 38).

- **He would not beg for his life.** He refused to weep before the jury or parade his children, saying it would disgrace him and the city and ask the judges to break their oath (_Apology_ 34c to 35d). Xenophon adds that he could easily have been acquitted by a little flattery, and chose to obey the law and die (_Memorabilia_ IV.4.4).

- **He proposed a reward before a penalty.** Asked to propose his own punishment, he said a public benefactor deserved free meals in the Prytaneum, the honor given to Olympic victors. Only then did he offer a fine of one mina, raised to thirty on the guarantee of Plato, Crito, and others (_Apology_ 36d to 38b).

- **He refused to fear death.** To fear death is to think you know what you do not; no one knows whether it is the greatest good. He knew only that doing wrong and disobeying a better is evil (_Apology_ 29a to 29b).

- **He met his sentence without bitterness.** He told the jury no evil can happen to a good man in life or in death (_Apology_ 41c to 41d). Xenophon reports he bore his last days with a cheerfulness that astonished his friends (_Memorabilia_ IV.8.2).

### Modern examples to strive for

- **Be the one who says no in the room.** When a meeting drifts toward something unfair or dishonest, say so plainly, even if you are the only one, as Socrates was at the trial of the generals.

- **Refuse the unethical order quietly.** If a boss or client asks you to falsify a report, mislead a customer, or cut a corner that could hurt someone, decline and go home, the way Socrates did with Leon of Salamis. You do not need a speech.

- **Stay at your post when it gets hard.** Keep showing up for the difficult obligation: the aging parent, the struggling marriage, the project everyone else has abandoned. Staying is not submitting to abuse; leaving a harmful relationship is consistent with everything here.

- **Tell the truth to people with power over you.** Give an honest assessment to a manager, a board, or a client who would rather hear something else.

- **Do not beg for approval.** In a performance review, a conflict, or an online argument, make your case honestly and let it stand without flattery or self-abasement.

- **Face the diagnosis, the layoff, or the loss without pretending to know it is the worst thing.** Take the next right step and leave the rest open, as Socrates did about death.

- **Keep your practice when it costs you.** Whatever your version of philosophy is, whether honesty, faith, or principle, do not drop it just because it has become inconvenient or unpopular.

## Self-discipline and temperance

- **He mastered his appetites and endured hardship.** Xenophon calls him the most self-controlled of men in matters of sex and food, able to endure winter cold and summer heat and every kind of toil, and trained to need so little that he always had enough (_Memorabilia_ I.2.1).

- **He lived on almost nothing.** Antiphon the sophist mocked him for eating poor food, wearing one cloak winter and summer, and going barefoot. Socrates answered that hunger is the best sauce, that he trained his body to bear whatever came, and that the greatest pleasure is knowing you are becoming better and making better friends (_Memorabilia_ I.6.2 to 10).

- **He ate and drank only for need.** He ate only as much as he could eat with pleasure and came to meals hungry enough that appetite was his seasoning (_Memorabilia_ I.3.5 to 6).

- **He took no money for his company.** He called those who charged for their conversation sellers of themselves, and valued his freedom to talk with whomever he chose (_Memorabilia_ I.2.5 to 7, I.6.5).

- **He warned against the pull of beauty.** When Critobulus kissed a handsome youth, Socrates told Xenophon that a kiss is more dangerous than a tarantula's bite and advised him to run from such beauty without looking back. For himself, Xenophon says, he was so well armed that abstaining from the most beautiful was easier for him than for others to abstain from the plainest (_Memorabilia_ I.3.8 to 15).

- **He rebuked Critias in public.** When Critias, later the most violent of the Thirty, kept pursuing young Euthydemus, Socrates told him it was unworthy of a free man, and when that failed, mocked his desire before the company. Critias never forgave him (_Memorabilia_ I.2.29 to 31).

- **He taught self-control as the foundation of virtue.** He asked what man could learn or practice anything good without self-control, and called the self-controlled man free and the slave to pleasure the least free of all (_Memorabilia_ I.5, IV.5).

- **He poked fun at gluttony.** At shared dinners he teased the man who ate his relish without bread, and warned that mixing many dainties in one mouthful spoils them all (_Memorabilia_ III.14).

- **He never chose the pleasant over the better.** Xenophon's final judgment: in all his life he never preferred what was sweeter to what was better (_Memorabilia_ IV.8.11).

### Modern examples to strive for

- **Put the phone down.** Treat the endless scroll the way Socrates treated the tempting kiss: the pull is stronger than it looks, so keep your distance rather than trusting yourself to resist up close.

- **Eat when hungry and stop when satisfied.** Let appetite be the seasoning, and skip the snacking, the second helping, and the drink you do not really want.

- **Train for discomfort on purpose.** Take the cold shower, the hard workout, the long walk in bad weather, so that hardship does not rule you when it comes uninvited.

- **Want less.** Wear the same few clothes, keep the old car running, and measure wealth by how little you need, as Socrates answered Antiphon.

- **Do not sell your time or judgment to the highest bidder.** Turn down the job, client, or sponsorship that would make you say what you do not believe.

- **Choose the better over the sweeter.** Pick the book over the show, the early morning over the late night, and the hard conversation over the easy avoidance.

- **Hold friends to a standard, kindly.** When a friend is pursuing something that will hurt them or someone else, say so, as Socrates did with Critias, even if it costs you the friendship.

## Wisdom

- **He knew that he did not know.** When the oracle at Delphi told Chaerephon that no one was wiser than Socrates, he set out to prove it wrong. He questioned politicians, poets, and craftsmen and found that each thought he knew things he did not. He concluded that his only wisdom was knowing the limits of his knowledge, and that the god meant human wisdom is worth little or nothing (_Apology_ 21a to 23b).

- **He kept examining, whatever it cost him.** He called this questioning his service to the god, and it earned him enemies and poverty, but he would not give it up (_Apology_ 22e to 23c).

- **He held the unexamined life not worth living.** Rather than go quietly into exile, he told the jury that daily talk about virtue is the greatest good for a man, and that life without such examination is not worth living (_Apology_ 38a).

- **He called himself the city's gadfly.** He compared himself to a gadfly sent by the god to wake a great but sluggish horse, reminding each citizen to care for his soul before his wealth. He called this work a mission from the god, the reason he could not stop (_Apology_ 30a to 30e).

- **He humbled a would-be statesman.** Young Glaucon, not yet twenty, was eager to lead Athens. Socrates gently asked him about the city's revenues, expenses, army, silver mines, and grain supply, until Glaucon saw he knew none of it and should learn before he led (_Memorabilia_ III.6).

- **He urged a capable man into public life.** Charmides was able but afraid to speak before the Assembly. Socrates showed him he already outargued its leaders in private, and told him not to be ignorant of himself (_Memorabilia_ III.7).

- **He brought a proud reader to know himself.** Euthydemus had collected the books of poets and philosophers and thought himself wise. Through questions about justice, Socrates led him to see he could not define what he thought he knew, and pointed him to the Delphic command to know yourself (_Memorabilia_ IV.2).

- **He held that virtue is knowledge.** He did not separate wisdom from self-control, and said justice and every other virtue is wisdom, since all fine actions are done by those who know (_Memorabilia_ III.9.4 to 5).

- **He fitted his teaching to each person.** He tamed those proud of their natural gifts by showing that the best natures most need training, and treated the rich, the shy, and the arrogant differently (_Memorabilia_ IV.1).

- **He limited study to what is useful.** He encouraged enough geometry to measure land and enough astronomy to tell the seasons, but warned against studies that consume a life and yield nothing for living well (_Memorabilia_ IV.7).

### Modern examples to strive for

- **Say "I don't know" out loud.** In a meeting, with your kids, or with a client, admit the limits of your knowledge instead of bluffing. It is the beginning of Socratic wisdom.

- **Check your own confidence.** Before you post, argue, or decide, ask the questions Socrates asked Glaucon: do I actually know the numbers, the history, the facts behind my opinion?

- **Examine your day.** Keep a journal or an evening review asking what you did well, where you fell short, and what you will do differently, so that your life is examined and not just lived.

- **Ask questions instead of lecturing.** When someone you are mentoring is headed the wrong way, ask them to walk you through their reasoning, the way Socrates led Euthydemus to see for himself.

- **Encourage the capable but timid.** Notice the colleague or student who knows more than they let on, and push them forward, as Socrates did with Charmides.

- **Learn what is useful for living well.** Spend your study time on what makes you a better person, parent, and citizen, not on trivia or endless information you will never use.

- **Be the gadfly your community needs.** Kindly and persistently remind the people around you of what matters more than money and status.

## Justice: how he treated people

- **He never wronged anyone.** Near death he said he knew he would be remembered as one who never wronged any man or made anyone worse, but always tried to make his companions better (_Memorabilia_ IV.8.10).

- **He showed justice by deeds, not definitions.** When the sophist Hippias demanded his definition of justice, Socrates answered that he showed it in what he did, and asked whether Hippias had ever seen him bear false witness, inform on anyone, or stir up quarrels among friends or strife in the city. Hippias admitted he had not (_Memorabilia_ IV.4.10 to 11).

- **He would rather suffer wrong than do it.** Against Polus and Callicles he argued that doing injustice is worse than suffering it, and escaping punishment worst of all (_Gorgias_ 469b to 469c, 474b to 479e).

- **He kept the law even when it killed him.** He obeyed the laws in private life and in office, refused unlawful orders from the Assembly and the Thirty, and at his trial would do nothing outside the law, preferring to die lawfully than live by breaking it (_Memorabilia_ IV.4.1 to 4).

- **He taught for free and stayed poor.** He never took a fee from anyone, rich or poor, and his poverty was his witness that he spent his life on the city's good (_Apology_ 31b to 31c, 33a to 33b; _Memorabilia_ I.2.5 to 7).

- **He helped a friend feed a household of fourteen.** When civil war left Aristarchus with fourteen sisters, nieces, and cousins to support and no income, Socrates advised him to let them work at the skills they had. The women became productive and happy, and the household grew fond of him again (_Memorabilia_ II.7).

- **He found honest work for a friend fallen on hard times.** He advised Eutherus, who had lost his property abroad and was living by manual labor, to find steady work as a steward before his strength gave out (_Memorabilia_ II.8).

- **He protected Crito from blackmailers.** He suggested Crito take on Archedemus, an honest but poor speaker, to defend him from informers, and Archedemus became a loyal friend (_Memorabilia_ II.9).

- **He urged friends to help each other.** He persuaded Diodorus that helping their struggling friend Hermogenes was worth more than saving a runaway slave (_Memorabilia_ II.10).

- **He taught a son to honor his mother.** When his own son Lamprocles complained of his mother's harsh temper, Socrates showed him how much she had done for him and that no one would trust a man ungrateful to his parents (_Memorabilia_ II.2).

- **He reconciled two quarreling brothers.** He told Chaerecrates to make the first kind move toward his brother Chaerephon, just as the hands and feet were made to help each other (_Memorabilia_ II.3).

- **He asked people to weigh their worth as friends.** He urged his companions to ask what they were worth to their friends, and taught that good friends are the most valuable possession (_Memorabilia_ II.4 to 6).

- **He checked anger at a slave.** When a man beat his servant as lazy and greedy, Socrates asked whether he had ever considered which of the two deserved the beating more (_Memorabilia_ III.13.4).

- **He bore his judges no anger.** He told them he was not very angry with those who condemned him, though they deserved blame for meaning him harm, and asked only that they correct his sons as he had corrected them if they cared for money before virtue (_Apology_ 41d to 42a).

### Modern examples to strive for

- **Let your record speak.** Aim to be someone about whom no one can say you lied about them, spread gossip, or set friends against each other, the test Socrates put to Hippias.

- **Take the loss rather than cheat.** Accept losing the deal, the argument, or the promotion before winning it by deceit or cruelty.

- **Help a friend solve the real problem.** When someone is struggling with money, work, or family, think with them about practical steps, as Socrates did for Aristarchus and Eutherus, not just offer sympathy.

- **Make the introduction.** Connect the person who needs help with the person who can give it, the way Socrates brought Crito and Archedemus together.

- **Honor your parents, even the difficult ones.** Remember what they gave you before you list what they got wrong, as Socrates taught Lamprocles. But obey them in nothing wrong (Musonius, Lectures XVI), and honoring a parent never means accepting abuse.

- **Make the first move toward reconciliation.** Call the sibling, old friend, or colleague you have fallen out with. Do not wait for them to go first.

- **Watch how you treat those under you.** Before you lose your temper with an employee, a waiter, or your kids, ask whether you are the one who needs correcting.

- **Give your knowledge freely.** Mentor, teach, and share what you know with people who cannot pay you back.

- **Forgive the people who wronged you.** Let go of resentment toward those who have harmed you, and still ask the people around you to hold your children to a high standard.

### The Stoic widening

The Stoics built on Socrates's justice with a doctrine they called _oikeiosis_: the natural affection every creature has for itself, which reason extends outward to family, city, and finally all humanity. Socrates pointed the way. Asked his country, he never said Athenian or Corinthian, but a citizen of the world (Epictetus, _Discourses_ I.9). Marcus put it in one line: people exist for one another; teach them, then, or bear with them (_Meditations_ VIII.59). Musonius compared human beings to bees, which cannot live alone, and defined virtue as brotherly love, justice, and concern for your neighbor's welfare (_Lectures_ XIV). Self-mastery is not the goal in itself. It is what makes you useful to others.

## A Socratic life in modern terms

Neither Socrates nor the Stoics made rules for every activity. They gave a way of judging, and then applied it to whatever life brought. This section runs the ordinary activities of modern life through that judgment. The verdicts are **Practice** (build it into your life), **In moderation** (fine within limits, dangerous past them), and **Avoid** (as a habit, inappropriate for someone training toward virtue). These are verdicts on actions, what the Stoics called appropriate and inappropriate acts, not on things. Circumstances can change what is appropriate, and the verdicts describe the ordinary case.

### Six tests

Every verdict below comes from one or more of these questions.

1. **Better or sweeter?** Does this make me better, or only feel good? Socrates never chose the sweeter over the better (_Memorabilia_ IV.8.11).

2. **Master or slave?** Can I take it or leave it, or does it pull me? The Stoics called the person ruled by pleasure the least free of all (_Memorabilia_ IV.5).

3. **Does it keep my judgment clear?** Reason is the one thing wholly ours. Whatever clouds it gives away the self (Epictetus, _Handbook_ 1).

4. **Is it up to me?** Put effort into what I control, my choices, and not into what I don't, like others' opinions or the news (Epictetus, _Handbook_ 1).

5. **How does it treat people?** Justice asks whether an activity uses, harms, or neglects someone.

6. **Would I do it with someone I respect watching?** Seneca passed on Epicurus's advice to live as if a man you respect were watching, and suggested Cato (_Letters_ 11). Pick your own: Socrates, your wife, your children.

### Diet

Socrates gave the rule that settles most of modern eating: avoid any food that tempts you to eat when you are not hungry, and any drink that tempts you to drink when you are not thirsty (_Memorabilia_ I.3.6). Musonius Rufus, who wrote more on food than any other Stoic, called eating the hardest pleasure to fight, because it tempts us every day and usually twice a day. He favored cheap, plentiful food, eaten for nourishment rather than pleasure (_Lectures_ XVIIIA and XVIIIB). Epictetus added that spending much time on eating is a sign of a dull mind, since the body should be tended in passing (_Handbook_ 41). Eating animals is treated separately below as an open question.

A note on the clinical side: if you have a history of disordered eating, read the fasting and restriction rows with a doctor or counselor, not alone. Stoic training aims at freedom from the body's demands, and restriction that becomes its own compulsion is the opposite of that freedom.

### Attention and screens

The Stoics had no phones, but they knew the problem. Epictetus taught that the one thing wholly ours is how we use our impressions, and every screen is a machine for supplying impressions faster than we can examine them.

|Activity|Verdict|Strive for|How not to act|
|---|---|---|---|
|Social media|Only as a tool|Use it as a tool with a purpose: post the work, answer the message, close the app. Marcus noted how much time you gain by not looking to see what your neighbor said or did (_Meditations_ IV.18)|Scrolling to fill every gap; posting for approval; measuring your worth in likes; comparing your life to other people's highlights|

|Gambling|Avoid|Take your risks on work and people, where effort matters|Betting for the thrill or to get rich; it hands your outcome to chance and trains the itch|

|Business dealings|Practice honesty|Tell the whole truth about what you sell; keep your word when it costs you|Misleading customers, fine print, or promises you know you won't keep; winning a deal by deceit, which Socrates held worse than losing it|

### Habits, tools, and hard things

### Small habits

None of these is a grave vice on its own. Each is a place where the body acts without your choosing, which makes them the best ground for training attention. The method is the same for all of them: never feed the habit once, and count the days, as Epictetus told the angry man to count the days he was not angry (_Discourses_ II.18).

In Stoic terms, the habits themselves are indifferents. A cracked knuckle or a twirl of hair is not a vice. The fault is in acting without choosing, and the verdicts below are about that habit of inattention, not about the knuckle.

Some reactions are not habits at all. Seneca says training can never remove a blush, a faltering voice, or trembling lips, even in the strongest men (_Letters_ 11). The Stoics called these first movements: they come without assent, and even the sage has them. The verdicts below apply to what you do next, not to the jolt itself.

A note on the clinical side: for some people, hair pulling (trichotillomania) and skin picking (excoriation disorder) are recognized medical conditions, not simple lapses of attention. The standard here does not change, but a habit that has resisted steady effort deserves a doctor's help, just as Epictetus would have you bear a fever well and still let the physician in (_Discourses_ III.10).

### A daily rhythm

- **Morning.** Get up when it's time. Prepare for the day by expecting difficult people and choosing in advance how you will act (_Meditations_ II.1, V.1).

- **Through the day.** Before each choice, ask the six tests, especially better or sweeter and master or slave.

- **Evening.** Review the day, as Seneca did each night: what fault did I cure, what did I resist, where am I better (_On Anger_ III.36)?

- **Weekly.** Go without something you take for granted, whether coffee, the phone, or a comfort, to keep it from owning you (Seneca, _Letters_ 18).

- **Always.** Remember that you are practicing, not perfect. Socrates spent his life examining and improving, and Xenophon counted him happiest because he felt himself growing in goodness (_Memorabilia_ IV.8.6).

## Feats to aim for

**Epictetus's rule comes first.** He warned against training for display, like a performer walking a tightrope. Train against your own weakness, and do it in secret: to train for thirst on a hot day, take cold water in your mouth, spit it out, and tell no one (_Discourses_ III.12). A feat done to be seen is vanity, not virtue. The goal is not to set records but to reach the point where conditions have no hold on you.

Push any physical feat sensibly. Extended fasting and serious cold exposure carry real medical risks, so bring a doctor in before you go far with either.

The passages from Plato's _Symposium_ below are not yet in the corpus. They are cited from general knowledge and should be checked against a translation before quoting.

### Feats of the body

- **Cold without complaint.** Socrates wore one cloak through winter and summer and marched barefoot over ice while other soldiers wrapped their feet in felt (_Memorabilia_ I.6.2; _Symposium_ 220a to 220b). Diogenes the Cynic hugged cold statues in winter to train for it (Diogenes Laertius VI.23).

- **Hunger with no hold on you.** On campaign he bore hunger and toil better than any soldier, and still enjoyed a feast when one came (_Symposium_ 219e to 220a). The feat is a fast done calmly and without complaint, not a test of how long the body lasts; he ate simply and only for need, and never treated his body as something to push to destruction.

- **Standing still in thought.** At Potidaea he stood from one dawn to the next, absorbed in a problem, then prayed to the sun and walked away (_Symposium_ 220c to 220d). It was not a stunt. It happened because the question mattered more than his comfort.

- **Composure under mortal danger.** In the rout at Delium he walked calmly off the field while the army fled, so steady that no one dared attack him (_Symposium_ 221a to 221b).

- **Fitness kept for use.** He fought at Delium in his mid-forties and danced alone in the mornings to stay fit (Xenophon, _Symposium_ 2.17 to 19).

- **Voluntary poverty.** Seneca advised setting aside a few days to live on the cheapest food and roughest clothes, then asking whether this is what you feared (_Letters_ 18). Cato went barefoot and bareheaded in all weather to harden himself (Plutarch, _Cato the Younger_ 5 to 6).

### Feats of character

These are the ones Socrates is remembered for, and they matter more than any feat of the body.

- **Refuse an unjust order when it could cost you everything,** as he did when the Thirty ordered him to arrest Leon of Salamis.

- **Stand alone against a crowd,** as he did at the trial of the generals.

- **Keep your principle when your life is the price,** as he did when he refused to stop philosophizing.

- **Refuse to beg, flatter, or perform** before people who hold power over you, as he did before the jury.

- **Resist the strongest temptation offered to you,** without strain, as he did with Alcibiades.

- **Meet death without fear or bitterness,** as he did with the hemlock.

- **Go a year without raising your voice.** This one is not in the sources, but it is the everyday form of everything above, and harder than any fast.

## Rules for living

The Stoics who followed Socrates turned his example into rules. This section gathers them, one teacher at a time, starting with Musonius Rufus, the teacher of Epictetus. Every rule below is checked against the source texts. For Epictetus these are the _Handbook_ (Higginson's translation of the _Enchiridion_, cited by the standard chapter numbers, since Higginson merges the last four chapters into two) and the _Discourses_ (Long's translation, cited by book and chapter).

### Musonius Rufus

Musonius has been called the Roman Socrates, and he was the teacher of Epictetus. He wrote nothing; his students recorded his talks. His rules come from those lectures (Cora Lutz's translation, cited by lecture number). More than any other Stoic, he gave rules for ordinary life: food, clothes, furniture, marriage, work, and children. Some of his rules on clothing and shelter lean toward the Cynic end of the school; Epictetus warned that the full Cynic life is a calling for very few (Discourses III.22).

#### Practice

- **Practice beats theory.** Would you rather be treated by a doctor who speaks brilliantly about medicine but has never healed anyone, or by one who cannot lecture but heals? It is better to be self-controlled in every act than to be able to explain what self-control is (_Lectures_ V).

- **Train body and soul together.** You are not a soul alone or a body alone. Train both at once with cold, heat, thirst, hunger, plain rations, hard beds, refusing pleasures, and patience under pain. The body grows hardy and the soul grows brave and self-controlled (_Lectures_ VI).

- **Keep the proofs ready.** Have at hand the reasons why the things that seem good are not good and the things that seem bad are not bad, and practice acting on them (_Lectures_ VI).

- **Build the five habits.** Do not love pleasure, do not avoid hardship, do not cling to life, do not fear death, and do not value receiving over giving (_Lectures_ VI).

- **Expect to need more training, not less.** Students of other arts start with clean minds. Students of virtue were raised among bad habits and must unlearn them first (_Lectures_ VI).

- **Look for a few strong reasons, not many.** A good doctor helps with a few effective remedies; a good student needs fewer proofs the sharper he is (_Lectures_ I).

#### Hardship

- **Remember what others endure for worse ends.** Lovers, money seekers, and fame seekers suffer willingly. Acrobats risk death for small pay. It is shameful not to endure as much for virtue (_Lectures_ VII).

- **Put your effort in the right place.** Instead of working to win another man's wife, work to govern your desires. Instead of working for money, train yourself to want little. Instead of chasing fame, work to stop wanting it. Instead of plotting against someone you envy, learn not to envy (_Lectures_ VII).

- **Accept that every good is won by toil.** The man unwilling to work hard shows himself unworthy of any good (_Lectures_ VII).

#### Insult and injury

- **Never sue over an insult.** Being reviled, struck, or spat on is no disgrace to the one who suffers it. The disgrace belongs to the one who does it. Bear it quietly (_Lectures_ X).

- **Do not bite back.** Plotting to return harm for harm is the act of a wild animal. Most wrongs come from ignorance and stop once the person is taught. Forgive, and be a source of good hope to the one who wronged you (_Lectures_ X).

- **Remember Socrates and Phocion.** Socrates, mocked on stage by Aristophanes, only asked whether he could be of use in another role. Phocion told the man who insulted his wife that his wife had suffered nothing (_Lectures_ X).

#### Work

- **Earn your own living with your own hands.** Supplying your own needs is more dignified than asking others for them. Farming is the most fitting living for a philosopher, and keeping flocks best of all, because light work leaves the mind free to think (_Lectures_ XI).

- **Teach by working.** Students learn more by seeing their teacher endure labor in the field than by hearing him lecture in the city (_Lectures_ XI).

- **Live, eat, and drink with good men.** From good men you learn good; mix with bad ones and you lose what goodness you had (_Lectures_ XI, quoting Theognis).

#### Sex, marriage, and children

- **Keep sex within marriage.** Sex is right only within marriage, and not even there when it is pure pleasure seeking. Adultery is the worst of all (_Lectures_ XII).

- **Hold yourself to your spouse's standard.** Allow yourself no liaison you would find intolerable in your wife. Men should not expect to be less self-controlled than women (_Lectures_ XII).

- **What you hide, you confess.** No one does these things openly. Trying to cover up an act admits that it is wrong (_Lectures_ XII).

- **Marry for character.** Do not look for family, wealth, or beauty. Look for a healthy body able to work and a character trained in self-control and justice, in both husband and wife (_Lectures_ XIIIB).

- **Share everything in marriage.** Husband and wife should hold everything in common, even their bodies, love each other in sickness and in health, and try to outdo each other in devotion (_Lectures_ XIIIA).

- **Do not use philosophy as an excuse to stay single.** Pythagoras, Socrates, and Crates all married. The home is the city's first defense, and marriage is its foundation (_Lectures_ XIV).

- **Welcome children.** Musonius argued against avoiding children and praised large families as a good to the city and a duty to the family (_Lectures_ XV).

- **Teach daughters philosophy as you teach sons.** Women have the same capacity for virtue as men, so they need the same training in it (_Lectures_ III, IV).

#### Parents

- **Obey your parents in everything right, and in nothing wrong.** Refusing a wrong order is not disobedience. Doing what is right, even unasked, is doing what a good parent truly wants (_Lectures_ XVI).

- **Win them over by conduct.** If argument fails, be the kind of son who is never contentious or quick to anger, controls his tongue and appetites, and gives up pleasures for his father's sake (_Lectures_ XVI).

- **Remember that no one can stop you from thinking rightly.** Philosophy is done with the mind, which no one can lock up. You need no worn cloak or long hair to practice it (_Lectures_ XVI).

#### Old age and death

- **Prepare for old age the way you prepare for youth.** The best provision for old age is the same as for youth: live by nature, doing and thinking what you ought. Wealth can buy pleasure but not cheerfulness (_Lectures_ XVII).

- **Learn to wait for death without fear.** Fear of death is what makes old age miserable, yet death is a debt everyone owes, even the best man (_Lectures_ XVII).

#### Food

- **Start self-control at the table.** Self-control in eating and drinking is the beginning and foundation of all temperance (_Lectures_ XVIIIA).

- **Prefer cheap, plentiful, natural food.** Grains and other plants, cheese, and honey; cooked grains and vegetables are fine too (_Lectures_ XVIIIA).

- **Eat to live.** As Socrates said, most people live to eat. Food is the medicine of life: choose it to nourish the body, not to please the palate (_Lectures_ XVIIIB).

- **Know the faults of the table.** Eating too much, eating too fast, wallowing in sauces, preferring sweet food to healthy food, serving guests worse food than yourself, and putting off duty in order to eat (_Lectures_ XVIIIB).

- **Refuse delicacies even when sick.** Zeno, ill, refused the delicate food his doctor ordered and said, "Treat me as you would treat my slave." Once you yield to delicacy, pleasure picks up speed (_Lectures_ XVIIIA).

#### Clothing, house, and furnishings

- **Wear clothes like armor.** Clothes and shoes are for protecting the body, not for display. The best ones make the body stronger, not softer (_Lectures_ XIX).

- **Feel some cold and some heat.** Do not bundle up completely. One garment is better than two, and going barefoot, where you can, is better than sandals (_Lectures_ XIX).

- **Build a house like a cave.** A house should keep out cold, heat, sun, and wind, and store food. Everything beyond that is waste. Spend money on people rather than stones, and gain friends by doing good (_Lectures_ XIX).

- **Judge every furnishing by three tests.** How hard is it to get, how useful is it, and how easy is it to keep? The cheap, useful, and easily kept beat the costly and fragile (_Lectures_ XX).

- **Prefer sickness to luxury.** Sickness harms only the body. Luxury ruins both body and soul (_Lectures_ XX).

- **Groom for function, not looks.** Cut the hair only to remove what is excess, as you prune a vine, and not to please anyone. Musonius also held that a man should keep his beard (_Lectures_ XXI).

### Epictetus

Epictetus divided the whole of training into three areas: what to desire and avoid, how to act toward others, and how to keep your judgment from being deceived (_Discourses_ III.2). The rules are grouped the same way, after the foundation they all rest on.

#### The foundation

- **Know what is up to you.** Your opinions, aims, desires, and aversions are yours. Your body, property, reputation, and position are not. Treat what belongs to others as your own and you will be hindered and complaining; keep to what is yours and no one can compel or harm you (_Handbook_ 1).

- **Test every troubling impression.** Say to it, "You are an impression, not the thing itself." Then ask whether it concerns what is up to you. If it does not, it is nothing to you (_Handbook_ 1).

- **Locate the good in the will.** The good of a human being is a right disposition of the will toward impressions, and nothing else (_Discourses_ I.8).

- **Want nothing that others control.** Your master is whoever can give or take what you want. To be free, wish for nothing and shun nothing that depends on others (_Handbook_ 14).

- **Aim to be free, not important.** Do not wish to be a general or a senator, but to be free; the only road there is disregard for what is not up to you (_Handbook_ 19).

#### Desire and aversion

- **Restrain desire for now.** Until you are secure in what is yours, desire nothing, and shun only the bad things within your control. Where you must pursue or avoid something, do it lightly and with moderation (_Handbook_ 2).

- **Remember what each loved thing is.** If you love a cup, remember it is a cup, and you can bear its breaking. When you kiss your wife or child, remember you kiss a mortal (_Handbook_ 3).

- **Never say "I have lost it."** Say "I have given it back." Hold everything you have the way a traveler holds a room at an inn (_Handbook_ 11).

- **Want events as they happen.** Do not demand that things happen as you wish; wish them to happen as they do, and you will go on well (_Handbook_ 8).

- **Behave as at a banquet.** When a dish comes to you, take a modest share. When it passes, do not grab for it. When it has not reached you, do not strain toward it. Treat children, spouse, office, and money the same way (_Handbook_ 15).

- **Do not envy.** If you want what another has, pay his price: the flattery, the attendance, the praise. If you would not pay it, you kept what he spent (_Handbook_ 19, 25).

- **Keep death before your eyes daily.** With death and loss in view, you will never think a mean thought or want anything too much (_Handbook_ 21).

- **Delay every tempting pleasure.** Make it wait. Picture the enjoyment, then the regret after, then the satisfaction of having refused, and choose the victory (_Handbook_ 34).

- **Do not wish the impossible.** Wanting your family to live forever, or other people to be faultless, is wanting what is not in your power (_Handbook_ 14).

#### Duties to others

- **Measure your duties by your relations.** A father is owed care and patience because he is your father, not because he is a good one. If a brother is unjust, keep your own side of the relation just (_Handbook_ 30).

- **Learn your duties from your names.** You are a human being, a citizen of the world, a son, a father; each name carries its own obligations (_Discourses_ I.9, II.10).

- **Play your part well.** You are an actor in a play chosen by another. Whether the part is short or long, poor or powerful, your business is to play it well (_Handbook_ 17).

- **Serve only with honor intact.** Help friends and country in any way that keeps your honesty and self-respect. A faithful, honorable citizen is worth more to a city than money or buildings (_Handbook_ 24).

- **Stand by your friend.** Do not ask an oracle whether to share a friend's danger. Reason already tells you to stand with him (_Handbook_ 32).

- **Say what is right, even at the cost of your life.** Helvidius Priscus told the emperor who threatened to kill him, "It is your part to kill; mine to die, but not in fear" (_Discourses_ I.2).

- **Count the cost before you begin.** Consider what comes before and after any undertaking. Otherwise you will start with enthusiasm and quit in shame, like a child playing at everything and nothing in earnest (_Handbook_ 29).

- **Do not take a part beyond your strength.** You will fail at it and neglect the part you could have filled (_Handbook_ 37).

- **Do right openly.** When you have judged that something ought to be done, do not hide it, even if the world misunderstands (_Handbook_ 35).

- **Honor God simply.** Offer what custom asks, neither stingily nor extravagantly, and follow willingly whatever happens (_Handbook_ 31).

#### Judgment and assent

- **Blame your judgments, not events.** People are disturbed not by things but by their views of things. The beginner blames others, the learner blames himself, and the finished student blames no one (_Handbook_ 5).

- **When provoked, buy time.** It is not the insult that provokes you but your opinion of it. Gain a moment and you will command yourself more easily (_Handbook_ 20).

- **Meet every event with a faculty.** Faced with beauty, use self-control; with pain, endurance; with insults, patience (_Handbook_ 10).

- **Separate the body from the will.** Sickness impedes the body and lameness the leg, but neither impedes the will unless it consents (_Handbook_ 9).

- **Take everything by the handle that can be carried.** If your brother wrongs you, do not grab the matter by his injustice. Take it by the fact that he is your brother, raised with you (_Handbook_ 43).

- **Say "It seemed so to him."** Whoever wrongs you acts on what seemed right to him. If he is mistaken, he is the one harmed (_Handbook_ 42).

- **Pity wrongdoers; do not hate them.** A thief is blind in the faculty that tells good from bad. You would not rage at a blind man. And you would not be angry at the thief if you did not overvalue what he took (_Discourses_ I.18, I.28).

- **Judge only what you understand.** Do not say a man drinks badly, only that he drinks a lot; you do not know his reasons (_Handbook_ 45).

- **Do not measure yourself by possessions.** "I am richer, therefore better" does not follow. You are neither your property nor your eloquence (_Handbook_ 44).

- **Grieve your own losses as you would another's.** When a neighbor's cup breaks you say such things happen. Say the same when it is yours, and when it is something far greater (_Handbook_ 26).

- **Do not hand your mind to anyone who insults you.** You would be angry if someone handed your body to a stranger. Why hand over your mind (_Handbook_ 28)?

- **Guard your mind as you guard your feet.** You watch where you step to avoid a nail. Take the same care not to injure your ruling faculty in every action (_Handbook_ 38).

- **Never let attention lapse.** Keep your principles ready, and do not sleep, rise, eat, drink, or converse without them. No part of life is done better by inattention (_Discourses_ IV.12).

- **Make every omen lucky.** Whatever happens, it is in your power to draw benefit from it (_Handbook_ 18).

#### Speech and company

- **Choose a character and keep it.** Set yourself a way of being that you hold to alone and in company (_Handbook_ 33).

- **Be mostly silent.** Say what is necessary, in few words (_Handbook_ 33).

- **Leave the common topics alone.** Do not talk much about sports, food, or drink, and above all do not talk about other people, praising, blaming, or comparing (_Handbook_ 33).

- **Laugh little and swear no oaths.** Let your laughter be neither loud nor frequent, and avoid oaths as far as you can (_Handbook_ 33).

- **Choose your company.** Avoid vulgar entertainments. However clean you are, bad company will soil you (_Handbook_ 33).

- **Answer slander with humility.** If told someone speaks ill of you, do not defend yourself. Say, "He did not know my other faults, or he would have mentioned those too" (_Handbook_ 33).

- **Do not tell your own war stories.** Your adventures please you more than they please your listeners. Do not play the clown, and show plainly that you dislike indecent talk (_Handbook_ 33).

- **Meet the powerful unmoved.** Before going to someone important, expect to be turned away or ignored. If it is your duty to go, go, and never say afterward that it was not worth it. Ask how Socrates or Zeno would behave (_Handbook_ 33).

- **At games and shows, want only what happens.** No shouting, no jeering, and little talk about it afterward (_Handbook_ 33).

- **Do not call yourself a philosopher.** At a dinner, do not lecture on how to eat; eat as you ought. Sheep do not bring up their grass to show the shepherd how much they ate; they turn it into wool and milk (_Handbook_ 46).

- **Be content to be thought a fool.** About externals, accept looking foolish. If someone tells you that you know nothing and it does not sting, you have begun (_Handbook_ 13, 46).

- **Expect to be mocked when you start.** People will sneer at your new seriousness. Hold steady, and those who laughed will come to respect you (_Handbook_ 22).

#### The body and possessions

- **Give the body only what it needs.** Food, drink, clothing, house, and household only as far as need requires; cut off everything for show or luxury (_Handbook_ 33).

- **Let the body be the measure.** The body measures what you should own as the foot measures the shoe. Go past the measure and there is no stopping; the shoe becomes gilded, then purple, then jeweled (_Handbook_ 39).

- **Tend the body in passing.** Spending much time on exercise, eating, drinking, and the body's other functions is a sign of a dull mind. Give your main strength to reason (_Handbook_ 41).

- **Be chaste, without judging others.** Keep yourself pure before marriage as far as you can, but do not be harsh with those who are not, and do not boast of your own conduct (_Handbook_ 33).

- **Train in secret.** If you live frugally, do not boast of it. If you train yourself in hardship, do it for yourself and not for show: when you are very thirsty, rinse your mouth with water and tell no one (_Handbook_ 47).

- **Take the smaller share.** At a shared meal, the largest portion suits the appetite but not the fellowship (_Handbook_ 36).

- **Bear illness as a test.** Fever, thirst, and hunger are the time to show what your training was for. Bear them without blaming God or man (_Discourses_ III.10).

#### Training and progress

- **Feed the habits you want and starve the rest.** Every habit grows by the acts that match it. To end one, stop doing it and do something else in its place. Count the days you were not angry: every day, then every other day, then every third (_Discourses_ II.18).

- **Train hardest against your own weakness.** If you lean toward pleasure, lean past the middle the other way for training. If you are irritable, practice being insulted without being vexed (_Discourses_ III.12).

- **Begin with small things.** When a little oil is spilled or a little wine stolen, say, "This is the price of peace of mind" (_Handbook_ 12).

- **Be able to be alone.** Be sufficient for yourself and your own companion, with thoughts to occupy you (_Discourses_ III.13).

- **Treat your rules as laws.** Keep the rules you adopt as if breaking them would be sacrilege, and ignore what anyone says about it (_Handbook_ 50).

- **Stop postponing.** You are no longer a boy. Now is the contest and it cannot be put off. You are not yet Socrates, but live as one who wants to be (_Handbook_ 51).

- **Practice first, proof second, logic last.** The first thing is to not lie; the second, knowing why; the third, knowing why the proof holds. We do it backwards, and lie while proving that lying is wrong (_Handbook_ 52).

- **Know the signs of progress.** The one making progress blames no one, praises no one, and accuses no one. He looks to himself for all help and harm, smiles at praise, makes no defense against criticism, and watches himself like an enemy lying in ambush (_Handbook_ 48).

#### To keep at hand

Epictetus told his students to have these ready on every occasion (_Handbook_ 53):

- Cleanthes's prayer, "Lead me, Zeus, and you, Destiny," wherever you have set me; I will follow willingly, and if I would not, I would follow anyway.

- "If it pleases the gods, so be it" (Socrates to Crito).

- "Anytus and Meletus can kill me, but they cannot harm me" (Socrates at his trial). "Whoever yields properly to fate is deemed wise among men, and knows the laws of heaven" (Euripides).

### Seneca

Seneca wrote as a man still in training, writing to a friend in training. His rules come from the _Letters to Lucilius_ (Gummere's translation, cited by letter number) and his essays (Stewart's translation): _On Anger_, _On Benefits_, _On Providence_, _On Peace of Mind_, and _On the Shortness of Life_.

#### Time and death

- **Hold every hour in your grasp.** Do today's work today and you will depend less on tomorrow. While we postpone, life speeds by. Time is the only thing that is truly ours, and we let anyone take it (_Letters_ 1).

- **Live each day as if it completed your life.** Every day should be arranged as though it closed the series and rounded out your existence (_Letters_ 12).

- **Stop complaining that life is short.** Life is long enough if you know how to use it. We are not given a short life; we waste a long one on greed, useless busyness, wine, sloth, and ambition (_On the Shortness of Life_ 1 to 2).

- **Fear the thought of death, not death.** Death is always the same distance away. What we fear is our idea of it (_Letters_ 30).

#### The body and wealth

- **Treat the body strictly so it obeys the mind.** Indulge it only as far as health requires. Eat to end hunger, drink to end thirst, dress to keep out the cold, and live in a house that keeps out the weather; thatch shelters as well as marble (_Letters_ 8).

- **But do not torture it for show.** Living by nature does not mean being dirty on purpose, dressing in rags, or eating disgusting food to be admired. Seeking out dainties is luxury; avoiding ordinary, inexpensive things is madness (_Letters_ 5).

- **Measure wealth by desire, not by holdings.** The poor man is not the one who has little but the one who craves more. The right limit is first what is necessary, then what is enough (_Letters_ 2).

- **Live by nature, not opinion.** "If you live according to nature, you will never be poor; if according to opinion, you will never be rich." Nature needs little; opinion is never satisfied (_Letters_ 16, quoting Epicurus).

- **Rehearse poverty.** Set aside days to live on the cheapest food and roughest clothes, and ask yourself, "Is this what I feared?" Toughen the soul while fortune is kind (_Letters_ 18).

- **Keep exercise short.** Choose simple exercises that tire the body quickly and save your time for the mind (_Letters_ 15).

- **Never get drunk.** Drunkenness is madness taken on purpose; stretch it over several days and no one would doubt the man was insane (_Letters_ 83).

#### Fear and the mind

- **Do not suffer before the event.** More things frighten us than crush us; we suffer more in imagination than in reality. Do not be unhappy before the crisis comes (_Letters_ 13).

- **Change your soul, not your scenery.** Travel does not cure a troubled mind, because you take yourself along. As Socrates said, you travel with yourself (_Letters_ 28).

- **Find quiet inside the noise.** You are at peace when no noise reaches you and no word, flattering or threatening, shakes you out of yourself. Still, when you can avoid the uproar, avoid it (_Letters_ 56).

- **Welcome hardship as training.** God does not pet the good man; he tests him, hardens him, and fits him for himself. The veteran looks calmly at his own wounds; the recruit turns pale at the thought of one (_On Providence_ 1, 4).

- **Set your own limit on success.** Nothing frees you from hope and fear like halting of your own accord, before fortune chooses to stop you (_On Peace of Mind_ 10).

#### Anger and other people

- **Delay is the greatest cure for anger.** Ask anger for time, not to pardon the offense but to judge it rightly. If it waits, it ends (_On Anger_ II.29).

- **Ask whether you have done the same.** Before condemning, ask whether you have ever committed that fault or made that mistake yourself (_On Anger_ II.28 to 29).

- **Review every day before you sleep.** Ask what bad habit you cured today, what fault you resisted, and in what way you are better. Anger calms when it knows it must face a judge each night (_On Anger_ III.36).

- **Treat those below you as you would be treated by those above.** Remember that someone has as much power over you as you have over those who serve you (_Letters_ 47).

- **Give and forget; receive and remember.** The giver should forget at once that he gave; the receiver should never forget that he received. Never remind anyone of what you did for them (_On Benefits_ II.10).

- **Serve however you still can.** If public life is closed to you, serve in private. If you cannot speak, help with silent counsel. Socrates kept encouraging Athens under the Thirty (_On Peace of Mind_ 4 to 5).

#### Company, reading, and friendship

- **Avoid the crowd.** Seneca came home from the crowds and the games more greedy, more ambitious, more self-indulgent, and even more cruel. Nothing damages character so much as lounging at the shows (_Letters_ 7).

- **Judge a friend before, trust him after.** Think long about whether to admit someone to your friendship. Once you have, trust him as you trust yourself (_Letters_ 3).

- **Choose a guardian and live under his eyes.** Pick a Cato, or if Cato is too severe, a gentler Laelius, and picture him always as your protector and pattern. You cannot straighten what is crooked without a ruler (_Letters_ 11).

- **Read few authors, deeply.** Linger with a limited number of master thinkers and digest them. Everywhere means nowhere; the one who travels constantly has many acquaintances and no friends (_Letters_ 2).

- **Be able to stay put.** The first sign of a well-ordered mind is the ability to remain in one place and keep your own company (_Letters_ 2).

#### Rest

- **Alternate solitude and company.** Solitude cures us when we are sick of crowds, and crowds cure us when we are sick of solitude (_On Peace of Mind_ 17).

- **Let the mind relax.** It should not always be strained to the same pitch. Socrates played with little boys, Cato refreshed his mind with wine after affairs of state, and Scipio danced. Seneca even allows wine now and then to wash away cares, adding that moderation is wholesome both in freedom and in wine (_On Peace of Mind_ 17).

### Marcus Aurelius

Marcus wrote his rules to himself, as an emperor trying to stay a good man. They come from the _Meditations_ (Long's translation, cited by book and section).

#### The morning and the day

- **Expect difficult people before you leave the house.** Begin each morning by telling yourself you will meet the meddler, the ungrateful, the arrogant, the deceitful, the envious. They act from ignorance of good and evil, and they are your kin, so you cannot be harmed by them or angry with them (II.1).

- **Get up.** When you rise unwillingly, remember that you are rising to do the work of a human being. The plants, birds, ants, and bees all do their work. Were you made to lie under the blankets and keep warm (V.1)?

- **Do each act as if it were your last.** Put aside carelessness, pretense, self-love, and discontent with your lot (II.5).

- **Cut the unnecessary.** Most of what we say and do is unnecessary. On every occasion ask, "Is this one of the unnecessary things?" Cut unnecessary thoughts too, and unnecessary acts will not follow (IV.24).

- **Do your duty whatever your condition.** Let it make no difference whether you are cold or warm, tired or rested, criticized or praised, dying or doing something else (VI.2).

#### Death and time

- **Be good now.** Do not act as if you had ten thousand years. Death hangs over you. While you live and while you can, be good (IV.17).

- **Take troubles one at a time.** Do not think about all the troubles of your whole life at once. Ask of the present one, "What here is unbearable?" and you will be ashamed to answer. Only the present can hurt you, and it is small (VIII.36).

#### Judgment and the mind

- **Blame your judgment, then change it.** If something outside you causes pain, it is your judgment of it that disturbs you, and you can wipe that judgment out now. If something you are not doing troubles you, why not act instead of complaining (VIII.47)?

- **Dye your mind with good thoughts.** Your character becomes like your habitual thoughts, because the soul is dyed by them. Where a man can live, he can live well, even in a palace (V.16).

- **Stand like a promontory.** Be like the headland the waves keep breaking against, which stands firm and calms the water around it. Do not say "I am unlucky because this happened" but "I am lucky, because it happened and I remain unhurt" (IV.49).

- **Retreat into yourself.** You need no country house or seashore. Nowhere is quieter than your own soul, and you can go there whenever you choose (IV.3, VII.28).

- **Look within.** The spring of good is inside you, and it will keep flowing if you keep digging (VII.59).

- **Stand upright on your own.** Be cheerful and do not seek the calm that others give. A man must stand upright, not be held upright (III.5).

#### Other people

- **Be strict with yourself and forgiving of others.** Marcus never puts it in one line, but it runs through the whole book. When someone wrongs you, ask what he took to be good; then you will pity him, and it is your duty to pardon him (VII.26). The gods put up with bad men for ages; are you tired of it already, when you are one of them (VII.70)? What is asked of you is tolerance toward others and self-restraint in yourself (V.33).

- **Teach them or bear with them.** People exist for one another (VIII.59). If someone is wrong, correct him kindly and show him his mistake. If you cannot, blame yourself, or blame no one (X.4).

- **Do not demand that bad people not exist.** When someone's shamelessness offends you, ask whether a world without shameless people is possible. It is not, so do not require the impossible (IX.42).

- **Take revenge by not becoming like him.** The best revenge is not to become like the one who wronged you (VI.6).

- **Do good and move on.** Do not call people over to see the good you have done. Go on to the next good act, as a vine bears grapes season after season (V.6).

- **Do not wait for a perfect world.** Do what nature asks now, without looking around to see who is watching. Do not expect Plato's Republic; be satisfied if the smallest thing goes well (IX.29).

#### Character

- **Stop talking about the good man and be one.** No more discussing what a good man should be. Be one (X.16).

- **If it is not right, do not do it; if it is not true, do not say it** (XII.17).

- **Do not let power change you.** Do not be "made into a Caesar" or dyed with that dye. Keep yourself simple, good, pure, serious, unaffected, just, reverent, kind, affectionate, and strenuous in doing what is right. Honor God and help people. Life is short (VI.30).

- **Do not show off your discipline.** From Rusticus, Marcus learned not to make a display of his self-discipline or his good deeds, and to avoid fine writing and speeches (I.7).

## Famous episodes from works not yet in the corpus

These come from Plato's _Symposium_, _Laches_, _Crito_, and _Phaedo_, and Xenophon's _Apology_. They are well known and widely cited, but they were written from general knowledge rather than checked against a corpus text, so verify them before quoting.

#### Courage

- **He saved Alcibiades at Potidaea.** Alcibiades says Socrates rescued him when he was wounded, and then insisted the prize for valor go to Alcibiades instead of himself (_Symposium_ 220d to 220e).

- **He kept his head in the retreat from Delium.** While the army fled, Socrates walked calmly with Laches, looking so formidable that no one dared attack them (_Symposium_ 221a to 221b; _Laches_ 181b).

- **He drank the hemlock calmly.** He comforted his weeping friends, took the cup without trembling, and his last words asked Crito to pay a debt of a rooster to Asclepius (_Phaedo_ 116b to 118a).

#### Self-discipline

- **He walked barefoot on the ice at Potidaea.** In a bitter winter, when other soldiers wrapped their feet in felt, he went out in his ordinary cloak and marched barefoot (_Symposium_ 220a to 220b).

- **He could drink all night and never get drunk.** At Agathon's banquet he outlasted everyone, then left at dawn to spend the day as usual (_Symposium_ 220a, 223c to 223d).

- **He resisted Alcibiades.** The most desired young man in Athens tried to seduce him, and Socrates declined, valuing Alcibiades's soul over his beauty (_Symposium_ 217a to 219d).

#### Wisdom

- **He stood thinking from one dawn to the next.** At Potidaea he stood lost in thought through a whole day and night, then prayed to the sun and walked away (_Symposium_ 220c to 220d).

#### Justice

- **He refused to escape from prison.** When Crito arranged his escape, Socrates refused, arguing that one must never return wrong for wrong, and letting the Laws of Athens make their case to him (_Crito_ 49a to 54d).

- **He preferred an unjust death to a just one.** When a friend lamented that he was being put to death unjustly, Socrates asked whether he would rather see him put to death justly (Xenophon, _Apology_ 28).
