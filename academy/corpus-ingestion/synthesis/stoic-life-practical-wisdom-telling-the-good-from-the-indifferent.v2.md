# Good, Bad, and Indifferent: The Stoic Art of Telling Them Apart

Every day brings choices that feel urgent: whether to take the better-paying job, whether to push back against someone who has slighted you, whether to spend money on something that promises comfort. The Stoics held that most of the things driving these choices are neither good nor bad in themselves. Sorting them correctly from what actually is good or bad was, for them, not a preliminary to wisdom but the exercise of wisdom itself. The question is not abstract. It is the question underneath almost every practical decision a person makes.

## Wisdom as the Governing Virtue

The Stoics placed wisdom at the head of the four primary virtues, alongside courage, justice, and temperance. Diogenes Laërtius reports the school's definition: wisdom is "the knowledge of things good and evil and of what is neither good nor evil" (Diogenes Laërtius, Lives VII.92). That formulation is worth sitting with. Wisdom is not defined as cleverness, or as knowledge of how the world works, or even as self-knowledge in a vague sense. It is specifically the capacity to classify correctly: to know what belongs in each of three categories. Courage is then defined in parallel as "knowledge of what we ought to choose, what we ought to beware of, and what is indifferent" (Diogenes Laërtius, Lives VII.92). The classification runs through every virtue. This is not incidental. The Stoics treated the virtues as unified: each one presupposes the ability to identify its proper domain, and that identification is the work of wisdom.

Marcus Aurelius puts the same point in compressed form. Confronted with the goods that the many pursue, he asks whether any of them can seriously compete with "prudence, temperance, justice, fortitude." If a person has first grasped these as genuinely good, he writes, he will not "endure to listen to anything which should not be in harmony with what is really good" (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 5.12). Marcus is not merely praising virtue in the abstract. He is describing a kind of cognitive stability: once the classification is sound, it resists distortion by whatever happens to look attractive at the moment.

## The Three Columns

The technical Stoic account of good, bad, and indifferent is laid out with unusual care in Diogenes Laërtius. Goods are things from which benefit results: virtue and virtuous acts, the good person, and what flows naturally from these, including joy and gladness as supervening states. Evils are vices and what partakes of vice. Everything else falls into a third column: "things which do not contribute either to happiness or to misery, as wealth, fame, health, strength, and the like" (Diogenes Laërtius, Lives VII.104).

The school was careful to distinguish two senses of "indifferent." Wealth, health, and reputation are indifferent in the sense that they are not themselves good or bad, yet they are not indifferent in the sense of being negligible. Unlike whether the number of hairs on one's head is odd or even, these things have the power of "stirring inclination or aversion" and so some are "preferred" and others "rejected" (Diogenes Laërtius, Lives VII.104-105). A preferred indifferent has what the Stoics called value: it contributes to living in accordance with nature, gives the virtuous person something to work with, and is reasonable to pursue when other things are equal. But it remains indifferent in the strict sense because it cannot by itself produce happiness or misery. Chrysippus and Hecato, Diogenes Laërtius reports, denied that pleasure is a good on the grounds that some pleasures are disgraceful and nothing disgraceful is good (Diogenes Laërtius, Lives VII.103). The same argument applies to wealth and health: "of which both good and bad use can be made" cannot qualify as unambiguously good (Diogenes Laërtius, Lives VII.103).

Epictetus frames the same structure from a different angle. "Life is indifferent: the use is not indifferent" (Epictetus, Discourses 2.6). The thing itself does not determine the value; the quality of the will brought to it does. This is a striking claim. It means that the same external situation can be handled well or badly, and that what matters morally is not the situation but the handling.

## Why the Classification Is Difficult in Practice

The three-column framework sounds clean. In ordinary life it is not. The difficulty is not that people lack the theory. Seneca observes that we frequently know perfectly well what we should do and still do not do it: "You know that friendship should be scrupulously honoured, and yet you do not hold it in honour" (Seneca, Letters 94.26). The problem is one of attention, not information. The classification has been learned but is not operative at the moment it is needed.

This plays out in recognizable ways. A person knows that a colleague's approval is an indifferent, not a good, and still spends an afternoon rearranging work to secure it. Someone knows that financial security beyond a certain threshold is a preferred indifferent at most, and still finds that an unexpected expense produces something closer to dread than to mild inconvenience. The framework has not become a working habit of perception. It sits in memory rather than shaping the moment of choice.

There is a further difficulty. Preferred indifferents have genuine value: they are not nothing. Ignoring them entirely would be its own error. The question is always one of proportion: how much attention does this preferred indifferent warrant, and at what point does attention to it begin to crowd out attention to what is actually good? Marcus Aurelius captures the danger: "these things, even though they may seem to adapt themselves to the better things in a small degree, obtain the superiority all at once, and carry us away" (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 3.6). The slide is gradual, which makes it hard to notice while it is happening.

## Wisdom Requires Practice, Not Only Argument

Musonius Rufus insisted that knowing the theory of a virtue and having the virtue are two different things. He compared the situation to medicine and music: "as the physician and the musician not only must master the theoretical side of their respective arts but must also train themselves to act according to their principles, so a man who wishes to become good not only must be thoroughly familiar with the precepts which are conducive to virtue but must also be earnest and zealous in applying these principles" (Musonius Rufus, Lectures VI). His argument runs through specific virtues: a person cannot become temperate by knowing that one should not be overcome by pleasures if he has no practice in withstanding them. He cannot become just by knowing that fairness matters if he has never exercised himself against greed. The knowledge is necessary but insufficient.

Musonius returns to this point by contrasting two kinds of precedence. Theory comes first in order: you cannot act well in a domain you have not understood. But "in effectiveness, however, practice takes precedence over theory as being more influential in leading men to action" (Musonius Rufus, Lectures V). The test of whether someone has genuinely grasped the distinction between good and indifferent is not whether they can articulate it but whether their behavior reflects it under pressure.

Epictetus makes a similar point through the image of digestion. The philosopher who announces his principles at every opportunity has not yet absorbed them: "sheep do not hastily throw up the grass to show the shepherds how much they have eaten, but, inwardly digesting their food, they produce it outwardly in wool and milk" (Epictetus, Enchiridion 46). The classification of good and indifferent shows up, if it has been genuinely internalized, in how a person acts at an ordinary dinner, in how they respond when something they wanted does not materialize, not in what they say about Stoic doctrine.

## Asking for Help with the Classification

The Stoics did not uniformly hold that the wise person makes every judgment in isolation. Seneca argues in Letters 109 that even a wise person needs outside counsel in matters concerning "state and home duties, everything, so to speak, that is mortal," just as a physician needs consultation or a pilot takes advice (Seneca, Letters 109.14). The argument is not that the wise person's reason is defective but that self-love and fear in moments of pressure can narrow vision: "men see farther in the affairs of others than in their own" (Seneca, Letters 109.16).

Marcus Aurelius gives the same counsel with characteristic directness: "if thou seest clear, go by this way content, without turning back; but if thou dost not see clear, stop and take the best advisers" (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 10.12). The instruction is not to take advice as a substitute for judgment but to recognize when one's own judgment is obscured and to seek clarification before acting.

Seneca in On Benefits goes further, comparing human goodwill to eyes that retain the power of sight but require light to function: precepts and reminders serve as that light, summoning attention to what is already known but not yet operative (Seneca, On Benefits 5.25-6.1). This suggests that the classification of good and indifferent is not a one-time achievement. It requires ongoing reinforcement, which is one reason the Stoics read and discussed their texts repeatedly rather than treating them as material to be mastered once and set aside.

## Where the Sources Pull Apart

The sources do not form a seamless whole on the question of indifferents. The standard Stoic position, as reported by Diogenes Laërtius, is that wealth, health, strength, and reputation are preferred indifferents: reasonable to pursue, but not goods, and incapable of contributing to happiness in the strict sense (Diogenes Laërtius, Lives VII.103-105). Posidonius, however, "maintains that these things too are among goods" (Diogenes Laërtius, Lives VII.103). This is a substantive disagreement within the school, not merely a terminological one. If Posidonius is right, then health and wealth are not merely preferred but genuinely good, and failing to secure them is not merely unfortunate but a genuine loss of something that matters.

Seneca's letters show a related tension. He argues for the priority of virtue and explicitly endorses the framework of indifferents. Yet his counsel to seek advisers, to care for friends, to attend to the demands of ordinary civic life gives considerable practical weight to things that the strict framework would classify as indifferent. His letters leave open whether this reflects a considered view that preferred indifferents warrant more serious attention than the label suggests, or simply the texture of practical counsel that cannot be squeezed into a technical vocabulary. Readers who want a settled Stoic verdict on exactly how much the preferred indifferents matter will not find one that all the major sources endorse.

## Practices for Telling Them Apart

The following are concrete exercises drawn from the sources, adapted for use now.

- Before a significant decision, write down what you stand to gain or lose in each column: goods (virtuous action, justice, honesty, courage), preferred indifferents (money, reputation, comfort, health outcomes), and rejected indifferents (what you are trying to avoid that falls in the same category). The act of writing forces the classification to happen explicitly rather than remaining implicit. Marcus Aurelius's instruction to examine what is before you applies directly here: "attend to the matter which is before thee, whether it is an opinion or an act or a word" (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 8.22, drawing on a common Stoic instruction).

- When you notice a disproportionate reaction to an outcome, whether relief that is too intense or distress that lingers longer than seems warranted, treat it as information. Ask whether the thing that went well or badly was actually a good or a preferred indifferent. The reaction itself is a signal about which column you were implicitly using.

- Seneca's point about reminders is worth taking seriously as a practice rather than a piece of ancient advice-giving. Pick one precept that you already accept in theory but do not act on consistently, and return to it at a fixed time each day for two weeks. Not to think about it abstractly, but to recall one moment in the previous day when it was or was not operative. This is closer to what the Stoics meant by practice than reading more philosophy.

- When you are about to act in order to secure something in the preferred indifferent column, ask: what kind of action is this? Epictetus's point is that the use is not indifferent even when the thing being pursued is. A person can pursue health through careful attention or through obsessive anxiety; they can pursue a good professional reputation through honest work or through performance. The virtue or vice is in the how, and that is always within your power to examine before acting.

- Take advice on practical matters without treating advice as a substitute for judgment. Seneca and Marcus both allow that outside perspective clarifies things that self-interest obscures. The practice is to seek a person who will tell you what they actually see rather than what you want to hear, and to bring them a specific question rather than a general request for reassurance.
