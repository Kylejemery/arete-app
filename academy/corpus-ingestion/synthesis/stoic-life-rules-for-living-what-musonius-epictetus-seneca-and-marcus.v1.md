# Do as You Teach: The Concrete Rules of the Roman Stoics

Philosophy, for the four great Roman Stoics, was not a subject to be studied but a life to be lived. Musonius Rufus taught in fields as well as lecture rooms. Epictetus drilled his students with maxims short enough to carry into a crisis. Seneca wrote to Lucilius demanding that every argument be tested against actual conduct. Marcus Aurelius wrote to no one but himself, setting down rules in private so he would not forget them in public. What each of them taught was, at bottom, the same insistence: that knowing how to live and actually living that way must converge, or philosophy has accomplished nothing.

## Practice Before Theory

Musonius Rufus, in Lecture V, puts the case with characteristic directness. He asks a young man to consider two musicians, one who can speak well about music and one who can play it. The young man agrees that the player is more useful. Musonius then turns to self-control: "In the matter of temperance and self-control, is it not much better to be self-controlled and temperate in all one's actions than to be able to say what one ought to do?" His conclusion is precise: theory comes first in order, because practice cannot be brought into proper form without it, but practice takes precedence in effectiveness, because it is more influential in leading men to action (Musonius Rufus, Lectures V). The same priority appears in Epictetus. In the Enchiridion he names three topics of philosophy: the practical application of principles, the demonstrations that support them, and the logical apparatus that holds the demonstrations together. He observes that students do exactly the reverse of what they should: they spend all their time on logic and neglect the first topic, the practical one. The result is that a student can demonstrate why lying is wrong while lying without hesitation (Epictetus, Enchiridion 51).

## One Norm, One Life

Seneca, writing to Lucilius in Letter 20, makes consistency the central demand. "Philosophy teaches us to act, not to speak; it exacts of every man that he should live according to his own standards, that his life should not be out of harmony with his words, and that, further, his inner life should be of one hue and not out of harmony with all his activities." He calls this the highest proof of wisdom: that deed and word should be in accord, that a man should be equal to himself under all conditions. The test is deliberately practical. He asks Lucilius to notice whether dress and house are inconsistent with each other, whether frugal meals coexist with lavish building, whether a man presents one face in private and another before the public. These are not decorative examples. Seneca treats them as diagnostic: inconsistency of conduct reveals a wavering mind that cannot yet keep its balance. His prescription is equally concrete: "You should lay hold, once for all, upon a single norm to live by, and should regulate your whole life according to this norm" (Seneca, Letters 20). He does not claim this is easy, or that the philosopher will always keep the same pace. He says only that the philosopher can always travel the same path.

## The Weight of Small Rules

Epictetus trusted maxims. In the Enchiridion he recommends that certain lines of verse be kept ready at hand for any occasion: the Stoic hymn of Cleanthes asking to be led wherever fate decrees, and the reply attributed to Socrates at his trial, "Anytus and Melitus may kill me indeed," accepting the outcome without rancor (Epictetus, Enchiridion 51). In the Golden Sayings, Favorinus reports Epictetus teaching that two words were sufficient to keep most people out of serious error: bear and forbear. "If a man will only have these two words at heart, and heed them carefully by ruling and watching over himself, he will for the most part fall into no sin, and his life will be tranquil and serene" (Epictetus, Golden Sayings CLXXXIII). Other precepts in the Golden Sayings are more specific in form: do not spend more time than necessary on the body (CLXXIII); when you have decided a thing ought to be done, do not shrink from being seen doing it (CLXXII); every difficult situation has two handles, one by which it can be borne and one by which it cannot, and the choice of handle is yours (CLXXIV). Marcus Aurelius records a related precept from Epictetus in the Meditations: that a person must find rules for giving assent, must make movements consistent with social interests and proportionate to the value of what is at stake, and must keep entirely away from sensual desire while avoiding aversion toward anything outside one's power (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 11.37).

## Rules Learned from People, Not Pages

All four writers treat the living teacher, or the remembered model, as more effective than any written precept. Seneca, in his essay on the happy life, argues that the best conversation is with philosophers who teach things rather than words, and that example has the force of a precept while touching the heart with an affection for goodness that mere argument does not reach. He contrasts this with philosophers who do not live as they teach, calling them a scandal to virtue. He cites the case of Cleanthes: "Cleanthes had never hit Zeno so to the life if he had not been in with him at all his privacies, if he had not watched and observed him whether or not he practised as he taught" (Seneca, Morals, Happy Life). Musonius took this further in practice. Lecture XI records his argument that students are not harmed, and may be helped, by meeting their teacher at work in fields rather than in formal lecture rooms. The teacher laboring with his own body demonstrates the lessons philosophy teaches about endurance and self-reliance more persuasively than any discourse. The precepts that matter most are few enough to be heard between tasks. Marcus Aurelius, in Meditations 1.7, credits his teacher Rusticus not with any doctrine but with the impression that his character required improvement and discipline, and with the instruction to abstain from rhetoric, display, and surface understanding of books. The lessons he lists are concrete habits: write letters simply, be quickly pacified when others show a readiness to be reconciled, read carefully rather than superficially.

## Conduct as a Present Matter

The practical force of these rules depends on treating them as immediate rather than aspirational. Epictetus says in the Enchiridion that the combat is now, that it cannot be put off, that by one failure honor may be lost or won (Epictetus, Enchiridion 50). This is not encouragement. It is a refusal to allow the student the comfort of intending to improve later. Most of us are skilled at constructing futures in which we will finally be consistent, patient, honest about our faults, moderate in what we consume. The Stoic move is to notice that this construction is itself the problem. The single norm Seneca demands cannot be adopted later. It can only be adopted now, and tested against today's inconsistencies between private conduct and public self-presentation, between what we say about money and how we actually spend it, between the values we claim and the choices we make when no one is watching. The question worth sitting with is not which norm to adopt, but whether you have one at all.

## The Two Handles in Ordinary Life

The image Epictetus offers of two handles is useful precisely because it applies before crisis, not only during one. A colleague behaves badly. A family member makes a decision you find foolish. A friend fails to show up when it mattered. Each situation has a handle labeled injustice or stupidity or betrayal, and a second handle labeled something else: this person is frightened, or overloaded, or doing what people like them tend to do. Neither handle is a lie about what happened. Both are accurate descriptions. The difference is that one makes the situation bearable and the other does not. Epictetus is not recommending naivety. He is recommending that you choose the handle by which a thing can actually be carried, on the grounds that the one by which it cannot be carried will simply be dropped, probably badly. The Stoics are not unanimous about what follows from this. Seneca's Letter 29 raises the question of whether you are obliged to keep advising someone who will not listen, and leaves it genuinely open. What the Stoics do agree on is that your choice of handle is yours, and that choosing poorly is not the fault of the situation.

## Marcus and the Portrait of a Model

Marcus Aurelius uses his adoptive father Antoninus Pius as a sustained model in Meditations 6.30 and 1.16. The portrait is built entirely from observable conduct: he never let anything pass without careful examination; he bore unjust blame without blaming in return; he did nothing in a hurry; he listened to no calumnies; he used fortune's gifts without arrogance; he was at peace with small accommodations in lodging, food, and dress; he tolerated free speech in those who opposed his opinions and took pleasure when anyone showed him something better. Marcus draws an explicit lesson: "Do everything as a disciple of Antoninus" (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.30). This is rule-giving by portrait rather than by precept. The effect is to make the standard concrete rather than abstract, visible rather than theoretical. In Meditations 1.16, Marcus observes that Antoninus overcame passion, released friends from the obligation to attend him, never checked his investigation through satisfaction with first appearances, and took reasonable care of his body without being attached to life or appearance. The list is long and deliberate, as if Marcus is building a reference he can consult. Whether this method of self-instruction worked for anyone other than Marcus is a question the surviving Meditations cannot answer.

## Practices

Each of the four teachers points toward something a person can do this week, not eventually.

From Seneca: identify one area where your private conduct and your public claims are inconsistent. Not as self-accusation for its own sake, but as diagnosis. Letter 29 recommends playing the accuser, then the judge, then the intercessor. The sequence matters. Accusation alone produces guilt without direction; the intercessor step asks what change is actually possible.

From Musonius: spend time with someone who does what they say. Not a mentor in any formal sense, but a person whose conduct and words fit each other closely enough that watching them is instructive. Musonius thought proximity to such a person more valuable than formal instruction.

From Epictetus: take the two words bear and forbear seriously as a daily practice. Before a difficult conversation, before reading news that reliably produces agitation, before responding to a message that seems unfair, pause long enough to ask which of the two applies. Some things must be borne. Some things must be refused. Many errors come from mixing them up.

From Marcus: identify one person whose conduct you genuinely admire, and write down what they actually do, not what they believe or value in the abstract, but the specific behaviors that make them worth watching. Return to that list when your own conduct drifts.
