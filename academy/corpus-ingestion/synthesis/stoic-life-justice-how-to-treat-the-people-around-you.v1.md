# Justice Toward the People Around You

Justice is the one Stoic virtue that cannot be practiced alone. You can cultivate courage in solitude, work on temperance in private, reason through philosophical questions in your own mind. But justice, from its earliest definition in the school, is about distribution: what you owe other people, how you treat them, where their claims on you begin and end. The Stoics inherited this from Socrates and then widened it in a direction that still presses on anyone who takes it seriously. The question this piece addresses is simple in statement and difficult in practice: how should you treat the people around you?

## What Justice Is, in Stoic Terms

The Stoic school placed justice among the four primary virtues alongside wisdom, courage, and temperance. According to Diogenes Laërtius reporting the school's general teaching, justice has equality and fair-mindedness as its particular subordinate qualities, and it governs what the Stoics called "just distribution" (Lives of Eminent Philosophers, 7.125–7.126). This is not a vague aspiration toward fairness. The Stoics held that justice, like the other primary virtues, is a form of knowledge: a settled, intellectual disposition that issues in correct action once genuinely acquired. Diogenes Laërtius also records that "justice, as well as law and right reason, exists by nature and not by convention," citing Chrysippus's work On the Morally Beautiful (Lives, 7.128–7.130). The implication is significant. Justice is not whatever your society agrees it is. It has a ground independent of local custom, a point Musonius Rufus pressed when he argued that no one can really understand justice without philosophical study, since people who have not studied it disagree constantly about where it lies, while things genuinely known produce agreement (Lectures, VIII). The disagreement itself, Musonius suggested, is diagnostic: it shows ignorance, not irresolvable difference.

## Socrates as the Model

Epictetus returned again and again to Socrates as the figure who showed what justice in daily life looks like, not as a theory but as a way of being in relation to other people. In the Discourses, Epictetus describes Socrates as someone who "not only himself on all occasions avoided fights, but would not allow even others to quarrel," citing Xenophon's Symposium as evidence (Discourses, 4.4–4.5). The explanation Epictetus offers is characteristically Stoic: Socrates "remembered well that no man has in his power another man's ruling principle." He therefore wished for nothing from others except that they be able to act according to their own nature. This is not passivity. It is a precise understanding of where your responsibility ends and another person's begins. Musonius Rufus pushed this further in his tenth lecture, arguing that to return wrong for wrong is characteristic of an animal incapable of reasoning, because most wrongs are done through ignorance, and the just response to ignorance is instruction, not retaliation. He illustrated the point with the philosopher Phocion, who, when his wife had been insulted and the insulter came begging forgiveness, replied that his wife had suffered nothing, so no apology to him was needed (Lectures, X). Musonius also observed, without sentimentality, that it is the wrongdoer who shames himself, not the one who bears the wrong.

## Justice at Every Scale: Slaves, Friends, Strangers

Seneca's writing on clemency and benefits extends justice outward from personal encounter toward those who are most easily forgotten or dismissed. In his discussion of clemency, Seneca argued that there must be "a tenderness to be used even toward our slaves, and those that we have bought with our money: how much more then to free and to honest men" (Morals, Clemency). The direction of the argument matters: he moves from the case where the power differential is greatest to the case of equals, treating the harder case first in order to establish the principle. Clemency, he insisted, is not weakness or indifference to wrongdoing. It requires discriminating between the curable and the desperate, and where the scales are balanced, letting mercy tip them. Marcus Aurelius stated the obligation more briefly but with equal force: "Everywhere and at all times it is in thy power piously to acquiesce in thy present condition, and to behave justly to those who are about thee" (Meditations, 7.54). The phrase "about thee" is worth holding. Justice is not first a political principle or a legal one. It operates in the immediate circle of whoever happens to be present.

## The Widening Circle

The Stoics did not leave justice at the scale of personal encounter. Diogenes Laërtius records from Zeno's Republic and from Chrysippus that the wise man feels affection for those who show natural endowment for virtue, and the school's cosmopolitanism pressed the obligations of justice outward toward anyone who shares in reason, which meant everyone (Lives, 7.129–7.130). This is not a minor amendment to an otherwise local ethic. It is a structural claim: rational nature, wherever it appears, generates a relationship that carries moral weight. Chrysippus in the first book of his On Justice drew the limit at non-human animals, holding that there can be no question of right between humans and other species due to their unlikeness. But among human beings, the community is in principle unlimited. How to act on that principle when the circle becomes vast enough to include millions of people you will never meet is a question the ancient texts address only partially.

## Where the Ancient Teaching Strains

In October 2026, Germany confirmed roughly 16,000 heat-related deaths across the summer, and catastrophic floods struck France, Spain, Greece, and Turkey within weeks of that announcement. The people who died in hospitals overwhelmed by heat, the families displaced by flooding: these are not strangers in any sense that the Stoic framework of universal community permits. They are, by the school's own cosmopolitan logic, people toward whom justice makes demands. Yet the ancient texts were written for individuals navigating their own lives within an existing natural and social order, not for populations confronting harms generated by the accumulated choices of generations across many societies. Seneca counsels treating everyone with humanity; Marcus says to behave justly toward those about you; Musonius says to absorb injury without retaliation. These are not useless counsels. But they address a different kind of problem. When the cause of suffering is diffuse, legal, and spread across centuries of industrial choice, the line between what is up to us and what is not, which Stoic ethics depends on, becomes genuinely unclear in a way that the Discourses and the Meditations do not resolve. The surviving texts leave this open. It would be a distortion to claim otherwise.

## Anger, Judgment, and the Person in Front of You

Whatever the scale of the world, the daily practice of justice begins in particular encounters. Someone speaks ill of you. A colleague takes credit for your work. A neighbor is thoughtless in a way that costs you time or peace. Seneca's On Anger offers a discipline for these moments: before responding, examine the disposition and purpose of the person who has wronged you. Is it a child? Pardon youth. A friend? Consider what has been given. Someone under compulsion? Then anger is simply unjust (On Anger, 2.10). This is not a technique for avoiding conflict. It is a structural habit of asking, before you react, whether you actually understand what happened and why. Marcus Aurelius returned to this in a single compressed instruction: "in every movement have respect to justice, and on the occasion of every impression maintain the faculty of comprehension" (Meditations, 4.13). The faculty of comprehension is what the Stoics called the capacity to hold an impression steady long enough to examine it before assenting. In practice, it means the pause before you reply to the message, the moment before you say what you feel to the person who has frustrated you.

## Benefits, Ingratitude, and the Limits of Law

Seneca devoted considerable effort to the question of benefits: gifts, services, and acts of help that cannot be captured by legal obligation. His argument was that making ingratitude a matter of law would destroy the very quality that gives a benefit its moral weight, the fact that it is given freely, before witnesses only in the loose sense that it registers in the character of both giver and receiver (Morals, Benefits). This has an immediate application. Much of what justice requires between people who know each other belongs to this category: the favor done without expectation, the help extended to someone who has no claim on you, the willingness to absorb a wrong without converting it into a grievance to be settled. None of these can be compelled, and the attempt to compel them corrupts them. The person who keeps a running account of what they are owed, by friends, family, or colleagues, is not practicing justice. They are practicing a kind of commerce dressed in the language of fairness.

## Practices

Several things follow from the Stoic account that are concrete enough to begin this week.

- Before responding to someone who has wronged or irritated you, apply Seneca's inventory from On Anger: is this person acting from ignorance, compulsion, misunderstanding, or weakness? The answer changes what justice requires of you.
- When you extend a genuine benefit to someone, do not register it as a debt. If you find yourself keeping track, notice that and stop. The act of keeping track changes the nature of what you gave.
- Epictetus's advice about company is pointed: you cannot spend sustained time with people who act badly without being drawn, gradually, in their direction (Discourses, 4.1–4.2; Enchiridion, 33). Justice to others requires that you take seriously who shapes your judgment and habits.
- Marcus Aurelius's instruction to attend to justice in every movement is not a counsel of perfectionism. It is a reminder that the scale of the world's problems does not excuse you from the encounter directly in front of you. The person in the room has a claim on your attention regardless of what is happening elsewhere.
- Where you have genuine power over others, whether as a manager, a parent, a landlord, or in any asymmetric relationship, Seneca's principle about clemency applies: where the judgment is in balance, let the more generous reading prevail.
