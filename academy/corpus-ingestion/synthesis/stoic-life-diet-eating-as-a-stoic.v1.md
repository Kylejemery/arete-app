# What Should a Stoic Eat?

Food is easy to dismiss as a minor concern, beneath the attention of anyone serious about philosophy. The Stoics disagreed. Musonius Rufus, whose two lectures on food survive and are among the most direct pieces of practical Stoic writing we have, treated the subject as foundational rather than peripheral. How a person eats, he argued, shapes the soul as surely as how a person reasons or governs anger. The table is not outside philosophy; it is one of its proving grounds.

## The Foundation of Temperance

Musonius Rufus held that 'the beginning and foundation of temperance lay in self-control in eating and drinking' (Musonius Rufus, Lectures XVIIIA). This is not a casual aside. He returned to the topic frequently and, by his students' account, emphatically, treating it as a question of genuine philosophical weight. His preferred foods were those natural to human beings: grains, vegetables, fruits in season, milk, cheese, and honey. Meat he considered a less civilised kind of food, appropriate for wild animals rather than for people who aspire to clear thinking. His reasoning had two branches. First, the exhalations rising from meat are turbid and 'darkened the soul,' so that heavy meat-eaters appeared slower in intellect. Second, human beings are, of all earthly creatures, nearest in kind to the gods, and should therefore eat food that is light and pure, so that the soul remains, as Heraclitus described it, 'clear and dry,' and therefore 'wisest and best' (Musonius Rufus, Lectures XVIIIA). The argument is not merely about health. It is about what kind of inner life a given way of eating tends to produce.

## Eating to Live

Musonius pressed the point further in his second lecture on the subject. Just as a plant receives nourishment that it may survive rather than for pleasure, 'food is to us the medicine of life. Therefore it is fitting for us to eat in order to live, not in order to have pleasure' (Musonius Rufus, Lectures XVIIIB). He invoked a saying attributed to Socrates: that most people live to eat, while Socrates ate in order to live. For Musonius, adopting the reverse of the common arrangement was not a deprivation but a realignment of priorities. The person who spends ingenuity devising pleasurable food has, in his account, already conceded something important to appetite. He noted that some people even wrote books on cooking whose aim was to increase the pleasure of the palate while ruining the health, and that those who ate luxuriously tended to have less vigorous health overall, their appetites constantly requiring sharp sauces or wine to revive themselves, 'like worn-out iron that constantly needs tempering' (Musonius Rufus, Lectures XVIIIA). Zeno of Citium, Musonius records approvingly, refused special food when ill and told his physician to treat him as he would treat a slave: 'surely a good man should be no more delicate than a slave' (Musonius Rufus, Lectures XVIIIA).

## Seneca: Resolutions, Limits, and the Problem of Restraint

Seneca's account of his own relationship to food is more personal and less systematic than Musonius's lectures, and usefully honest about failure. In Letters 108, he describes resolutions made in youth under the influence of his teacher Sotion and of Pythagorean ideas: he gave up oysters and mushrooms, which he considered not real food but 'relishes to bully the sated stomach into further eating'; he avoided perfumes; his stomach remained 'unacquainted with wine.' Some of these resolutions held for life. Others did not. He admits this directly: 'other resolutions have been broken, but after all in such a way that, in cases where I ceased to practise abstinence, I have observed a limit which is indeed next door to abstinence' (Seneca, Letters 108). He adds an observation worth pausing on: 'it is easier for the will to cut off certain things utterly than to use them with restraint.' Total abstinence, paradoxically, can require less from a person than moderate enjoyment, because restraint must be re-exercised every time. In the Happy Life, Seneca puts the principle in its simplest form: 'happy is that man that eats only for hunger, and drinks only for thirst' and 'that which is too little for luxury, is abundantly enough for nature. The end of eating and drinking is satiety' (Seneca, On a Happy Life). This is offered not as miserliness but as a description of freedom: someone who needs little from Fortune presents a smaller target to her reversals.

## Marcus Aurelius and the Model of Antoninus

Marcus Aurelius does not write about food as a topic in its own right. What appears in the Meditations is instead the figure of Antoninus Pius, held up as a model in Meditations 6.30. Among the qualities Marcus lists approvingly is that Antoninus was able, 'on account of his sparing diet, to hold out to the evening, not even requiring to relieve himself by any evacuations except at the usual hour' (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 6.30). The frugal diet appears here alongside evenness of temper, constancy in reason, and disregard of empty fame: it is one element in a portrait of a man who has nothing excessive in him. In Meditations 1.16, in a similar portrait of Antoninus, Marcus notes that his foster-father 'was not curious about what he ate, nor about the texture and colour of his clothes'; things that most people spend considerable attention on simply did not absorb him (Marcus Aurelius, Meditations 1.16). The implication running through both passages is not that food matters greatly, but that one's attitude toward it reveals something. A man absorbed in the pleasures of the table is, by that absorption, less available for what matters.

## The Habit Beneath the Meal

Epictetus approaches the question of food less directly but makes a point that connects eating to the whole structure of Stoic practice. In the Enchiridion, he instructs: 'when you have learned to nourish your body frugally, do not pique yourself upon it; nor, if you drink water, be saying upon every occasion, I drink water' (Epictetus, Enchiridion 47). The external practice is not the point. Boasting about frugal eating turns self-discipline into a performance, which replaces one form of appetite with another, the appetite for admiration. The discipline is internal and private. The Golden Sayings make a related point: 'any methods of discipline applied to the body which tend to modify its desires or repulsions are good, for ascetic ends. But if done for display, they betray at once a man who keeps an eye on outward show' (Epictetus, Golden Sayings). And elsewhere: 'it stamps a man of mean capacity to spend much time on the things of the body, as to be long over bodily exercises, long over eating, long over drinking' (Epictetus, Golden Sayings CLXXIII). Time and attention spent on the pleasures of eating are time and attention taken from understanding. At a banquet, the guidance is simply to eat as one ought, without lecturing others about how they should eat. Socrates, Epictetus notes in the Enchiridion, avoided ostentation universally; at table as elsewhere, the work shows in what a person does, not in what they say about themselves.

## What the Sources Do Not Settle

The sources leave several things open. Musonius Rufus makes a strong case against meat on grounds of both intellectual clarity and human kinship with the divine, but the other Stoic writers do not follow him there explicitly. Seneca records the vegetarian arguments of Sextius and Pythagoras sympathetically, noting that Sextius believed both that enough sustenance existed without blood and that 'a habit of cruelty is formed whenever butchery is practised for pleasure' (Seneca, Letters 108), but Seneca never commits to vegetarianism himself and frames his account as a report of what once inspired him rather than as a present doctrine. Marcus Aurelius's frugality concerns quantity and attention, not the type of food. The surviving texts do not resolve how far Musonius's arguments about meat were accepted by the broader Stoic tradition, nor does Musonius himself ground the case in the formal doctrine of preferred indifferents, though Diogenes Laërtius reports that the Stoics generally understood nature's first impulse in animals as directed toward self-preservation rather than pleasure, and that life according to reason is what nature intends for rational beings (Diogenes Laërtius, Lives VII.85-87). How exactly that principle bears on choices between foods is a question the surviving school texts do not answer directly.

## Eating Now: What This Looks Like in Practice

The Stoic position on food is not a diet plan. It is a set of orientations, and they apply with some force to conditions most people now live in, where food is abundant, heavily marketed, engineered to be difficult to stop eating, and surrounded by social pressure in both directions: toward indulgence and, equally, toward anxious control. Neither indulgence nor anxious control is what Musonius or Epictetus is pointing at.

Musonius's core claim is that the person who has not established any order in eating has left one of the most accessible levers of self-formation completely untouched. Food arrives several times a day. Each meal is an occasion where appetite either governs or is governed. This is not dramatic. It does not require renunciation. It requires a settled prior question: am I eating to satisfy hunger, or am I eating to extend a sensation? Seneca's observation about total abstinence being easier than restraint is worth taking seriously here. For some foods or patterns, a clean decision in advance is less costly than re-deciding every time under the pressure of appetite.

Epictetus's warning about display runs in both directions in the present. Someone who makes a point of their frugal eating, their careful food choices, their discipline at the table, has converted a private practice into a form of self-presentation, which is precisely the trap he identifies. The discipline, if real, requires no audience. The same applies to the reverse: someone who performs enthusiastic enjoyment of rich food as a form of sociability, eating beyond satiety to avoid seeming difficult, has also let something outside themselves set the terms.

Some concrete starting points, taken directly from the sources:

- Eat to satiety, not to pleasure. Before eating, consider whether hunger is the reason. This is Musonius's criterion and Seneca's formulation in the Happy Life.
- Prefer simple, available food over elaborate food. Not because poverty is virtuous but because elaborate food requires and reinforces the habit of treating eating as an end rather than a means.
- If you practise any food discipline, keep it private. Epictetus's instruction is explicit: do not announce that you drink water.
- Spend as little time and attention on food as the body requires. Time spent devising, anticipating, and discussing meals is time spent training appetite to expect attention. What takes the second place, in Epictetus's phrase, should actually take the second place.
- When social eating draws you into eating more than hunger requires, notice it without making a scene. Eating as one ought at a banquet, in Epictetus's formulation, is not a performance of restraint; it is simply eating as one ought.
