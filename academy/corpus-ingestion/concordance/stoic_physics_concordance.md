# Stoic Physics and Epistemology: A Retrieval Concordance

Editorial apparatus for the Arete corpus. Each entry names a Stoic technical term in Greek, states what it means, and then lists the English words that actually carry it in the translations held in this corpus. The purpose is lexical: a query about pneuma should be able to reach passages that say "vital breath," and a query about the cognitive impression should be able to reach passages that say "the perceptive presentation."

These entries are editorial, not primary sources. They are not attributable to any ancient author and no counselor may quote them as though they were.

---

## 1. Pneuma

Pneuma is the Stoic active principle: a fiery, breath-like body that pervades all matter and gives every thing its particular character. It is not a spirit in the later Christian or Cartesian sense, and this is the point most often lost. Pneuma is corporeal. The Stoics were thoroughgoing materialists who held that only bodies can act or be acted upon, so the divine, the soul, and the virtues are all bodies. Pneuma is a mixture of fire and air, in constant inward and outward motion, and its degree of tension determines what a thing is.

In the older English translations collected in this corpus, pneuma is almost never transliterated. It appears as spirit, breath, vital breath, fiery breath, warm breath, the vital principle, the fiery intelligent breath, and sometimes aether or the ethereal fire. Arnold's *Roman Stoicism* uses spirit throughout. Cicero, in Yonge's rendering of *De Natura Deorum* II, uses heat, fiery force, and vital heat for the same idea, since Cicero is himself translating Greek into Latin and reaches for *spiritus*, *ignis*, and *calor*.

The primary evidence is Diogenes Laertius, *Lives* 7.138 to 7.139, on pneuma pervading the whole cosmos, and Cicero, *De Natura Deorum* II, where the Stoic Balbus gives the fullest surviving statement of the doctrine. Alexander of Aphrodisias and Stobaeus preserve the technical detail but are not currently in this corpus.

Anyone comparing Stoic physics to a modern theory of mind should begin here and should notice what pneuma is not. It is a body that explains cohesion, growth, life, and reason. It is not proposed as an explanation of felt experience, because felt experience was not a problem the Stoics had.

## 2. Tonos

Tonos is tension, the inward and outward tensile motion of pneuma that holds a thing together and gives it whatever unity and character it has. A stone holds its shape because the pneuma in it is under a particular tension. A soul is strong or weak depending on its tonos, which is why the Stoics can speak of the virtues as a matter of tension and of moral failure as slackness.

English renderings in this corpus include tension, tonic movement, tensional motion, elasticity, strain, tone, vigour, and strength. Where a translator writes of the soul's tone or of a slack and unstrung soul, the underlying term is usually tonos or its cognates. Seneca's *firmitas* and *robur* occupy adjacent ground in the Latin.

The concept matters for any graded account of mind, because tonos is what the Stoic scale is graded by. It is a quantity, and things have more or less of it. That formal resemblance to graded modern theories is real, and the difference in what is being graded is equally real: tonos grades cohesion and organization, not felt experience.

## 3. The scale: hexis, physis, psyche, logos

The Stoics distinguished four degrees of pneumatic organization. *Hexis* is bare cohesion, the holding together found in stones and bones and metals. *Physis* adds growth and nutrition and is found in plants. *Psyche* adds impression and impulse and is found in animals. *Logos* or *nous* adds reason and is found in adult humans and in the cosmos as a whole. Each higher grade contains the lower.

Translators render *hexis* as tenor, cohesion, habit, state, condition, or holding. Tenor is the older and now unfamiliar choice and appears in Arnold and in Stock's *A Guide to Stoicism*. *Physis* becomes nature or the principle of growth. *Psyche* becomes soul, life, or animal soul. *Logos* becomes reason, the rational principle, or Word.

The evidence is Diogenes Laertius 7.138 to 7.139, with supporting material in Cicero, *De Natura Deorum* II.

This ladder is the single most suggestive point of contact between Stoic physics and any modern doctrine that distributes mind through nature, and it is also the place where the comparison is most likely to fail. The Stoic scale is a scale of pneumatic tension and functional organization. Nothing in the ancient evidence says that a stone at the level of hexis has the faintest degree of experience. A reader who wants to argue that the scale is a scale of experience owes an argument, and the texts do not supply one.

## 4. Krasis di holou

Total blending, sometimes complete mixture or interpenetration through and through. The Stoics held that two bodies can occupy the same place entirely, each preserving its own properties, so that pneuma is present at every point of the matter it pervades without displacing any of it. The stock illustrations are a drop of wine diffused through the sea and fire pervading iron.

Translators write total mixture, complete mixture, blending through and through, interpenetration, fusion, commingling, and sometimes simply mixture. Where a text describes soul and body as thoroughly mixed rather than joined, this is the doctrine at work.

The Stoics needed total blending because they were materialists who wanted the soul to be a body and still to be everywhere in the living body at once. It is the technical device that lets them avoid both dualism and the reduction of soul to arrangement. This is the doctrine that most resembles what a modern theory needs when it wants mind to be intrinsic to matter rather than added to it, and it is worth noting that the resemblance is one of structural strategy rather than of content.

## 5. Sympatheia

Sympatheia is the mutual affection of the parts of the cosmos: because the whole is one continuous body pervaded by one pneuma, what happens in one part is registered in the others. It grounds Stoic divination, the unity of fate, and the ethical claim that a human being is a part of a whole and should act like one.

English renderings include sympathy, fellow-feeling, mutual affinity, mutual attraction, concord, harmony, common bond, conspiring together, and the interconnection of all things. Cicero's Latin gives *conspiratio* and *continuatio*, which Yonge renders as conspiring together and continuity.

The chief passages are Cicero, *De Natura Deorum* II, and Marcus Aurelius, *Meditations* 4.40, 6.38, and 7.9, where the doctrine is compressed into practical injunctions to think of oneself as a limb of a single body.

Sympatheia is where Stoic physics does the most ethical work, and it is also where a careless comparison to modern holism does the most damage. The Stoic claim is about causal and organic continuity in a single living body. It is not a claim about shared experience.

## 6. Hegemonikon

The ruling part of the soul, seated in the chest, which receives impressions, gives or withholds assent, generates impulse, and is the seat of reason. The other parts of the soul, the five senses, the reproductive faculty, and the voice, are pneumatic extensions from it.

Translators write ruling faculty, ruling principle, governing principle, governing part, master faculty, the commanding faculty, the sovereign reason, and sometimes simply the mind or the will. Long's older rendering of Marcus uses ruling faculty consistently; Yonge and Gummere vary.

The evidence is spread across Diogenes Laertius 7.110, Epictetus throughout the *Discourses*, and Marcus, where the hegemonikon is the fixed reference point of the whole book.

For any comparison with modern theories of mind, the hegemonikon is where the Stoics locate what would now be called the subject, and its physical seat and pneumatic constitution should be kept in view. It is a part of a body, not a point of view added to one.

## 7. Phantasia and phantasia kataleptike

*Phantasia* is an impression, an alteration in the ruling faculty produced by an external object. *Phantasia kataleptike* is the cognitive or graspable impression: one that arises from what is, is stamped and impressed in accordance with what is, and is of such a kind that it could not arise from what is not. It is the Stoic criterion of truth and the foundation of the entire epistemology.

This is the single most important term in this concordance, because it is almost invisible under its Greek name in this corpus while the English renderings appear in hundreds of passages. Translators write presentation, appearance, impression, image, phantasm, and visum. For the cognitive impression specifically they write the perceptive presentation, the comprehensible presentation, the apprehensive presentation, the graspable impression, the convincing presentation, and the criterion. Cicero's *Academica* in Yonge's translation is the fullest surviving discussion and uses visum and comprehendible presentation. Diogenes Laertius 7.45 to 7.46 gives the definition.

The practical consequence runs through Epictetus and Marcus: the discipline of assent is the discipline of testing impressions before agreeing to them, and this presupposes that some impressions are accurate and that the difference is detectable. Any modern position holding that perception systematically misrepresents reality is not a friendly extension of Stoicism at this point. It is a direct challenge to the doctrine that makes Stoic practice possible, and the challenge should be stated as such rather than smoothed over.

## 8. Sunkatathesis

Assent, the act by which the ruling faculty agrees to an impression and thereby turns it into a judgment. Assent is the one thing wholly in our power and is therefore the hinge of Stoic ethics: passions are not feelings that happen to us but judgments we have assented to.

Translators write assent, approval, agreement, consent, and yielding. Where Epictetus speaks of not being carried away by an appearance, or Marcus of the power to withhold, this is the term at work.

The doctrine is compatible with fate through Chrysippus's argument that assent is co-fated: the cylinder rolls when pushed, but it rolls as it does because of its own shape. See *Heimarmene* below.

---

## 9. Logos spermatikos

The seminal or generative reason: the rational formulae contained in the cosmic pneuma that direct the development of each thing into the kind of thing it is. The cosmos contains the seeds of everything that will ever unfold within it, and each world cycle repeats them.

Translators write seminal reasons, seminal principles, generative principles, productive reasons, spermatic reason, and rational seeds. Marcus uses the phrase directly and Long renders it seminal principles.

The doctrine is the Stoic answer to why the world is orderly rather than merely lawful, and it carries the providential and teleological commitment that most distinguishes Stoic cosmology from any modern account of the same shape. Modern positions that distribute mind through matter almost never distribute purpose with it. The Stoics did, and the difference is not decorative.

## 10. Ekpyrosis and palingenesia

The periodic conflagration in which the cosmos is resolved into fire, and the regeneration in which it is reconstituted identically. The Stoics held that the same events recur in each cycle.

Translators write conflagration, the general conflagration, the destruction by fire, resolution into fire, renovation, restoration, regeneration, and renewal. Plutarch's polemics against the Stoics discuss the doctrine at length in a hostile register, which is worth noting when passages are retrieved from him.

---

## 11. Heimarmene and pronoia

Fate and providence, which for the Stoics are the same thing described from two directions: the unbroken chain of causes, and the rational care by which the cosmos administers itself. Nothing happens contrary to fate, and fate is not blind.

Translators write fate, destiny, necessity, the chain of causes, the order of causes, providence, forethought, and divine administration. Cicero's *De Fato* is the principal text and preserves Chrysippus's cylinder analogy at 42 to 43: the push starts the cylinder rolling, but the cylinder rolls as it does because of its own shape, and so assent is ours even though the impression is not.

The cylinder is the argument any account of a mind embedded in a larger order must eventually confront, because it is the Stoic attempt to keep responsibility without exempting the agent from the causal order.

---

## 12. Apospasma

A fragment torn off. Epictetus uses it for the relation between a human mind and the divine reason: you carry a portion of God within you. Marcus works the same idea as membership in a whole.

Translators write a portion of God, a fragment of God, a part of the divine, an offshoot, a portion torn from, a particle, and a piece of the whole. Marcus is usually rendered with part rather than fragment, as in the part and the whole, or a limb of the body.

The passages are Epictetus, *Discourses* 1.14 and 2.8, and Marcus, *Meditations* 2.1 and 12.26.

This is the doctrine that most directly resembles a modern position in which individual minds are derivative of one larger mind, and it is the place where the resemblance is closest to being a resemblance of content rather than of shape. It is also where the Stoic account is least worked out, since the texts assert the relation without explaining how the fragment is individuated from the whole.

---

## 13. The cosmos as a living rational animal

The Stoics held that the cosmos is itself a single living being, ensouled, rational, and identical with God and with Zeus and with nature. This is not a metaphor in the Stoic texts and should not be softened into one.

Translators write the world is a living being, the universe is an animal, the world is animate and endowed with reason, the cosmos is a rational creature, and the world is a living creature possessed of soul and reason. The word animal in these older translations means ensouled living thing and does not mean beast.

The evidence is Diogenes Laertius 7.142 to 7.143 and Cicero, *De Natura Deorum* II. The Platonic antecedent is *Timaeus* 30b, where the cosmos is constructed as a living creature endowed with soul and intelligence, and the Stoic doctrine is best read as a materialist rewriting of that passage rather than as an independent invention.

Any comparison between Stoic cosmology and a modern doctrine of a cosmic subject should be made against these passages and against the Timaeus, and should state plainly that the Stoic cosmic animal is providential and rational, which most modern versions are not.
