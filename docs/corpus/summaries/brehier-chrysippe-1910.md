## Why Stoicism was a synthesis, and Chrysippus's place in it (pp. 1–3)

Bréhier opens by warning against treating "the Stoics" of the ancient reports as a single fixed doctrine. The Old Stoa, from Zeno to Chrysippus, was neither a closed dogma nor a school that merely commented on its founder; unlike Epicureanism, which hardened into tradition, it set general directions and tolerated wide variety in detail, and so produced more original thinkers than perhaps any other ancient school without splitting apart.

His explanation is historical. Old Stoicism grew up while Alexander's successors were building a new kind of political body, the State, founded on the will of ruler and subjects rather than on the religious traditions that had held the city together. Bréhier sees in this period a general opposition between conscious reason and inherited tradition, and he reads the Stoics as setting out to fuse such opposed pairs in every field: philosophy and religion, natural law and civil law, universal empire and city. Neither side of any pair could any longer be given up. So the Stoics found the ground of law in the individual sage, recovered universal reason inside traditional religion by allegory, and placed an ordering principle within cosmic change. The contradictions critics have found in Stoicism, ancient and modern, follow from its being, on Bréhier's account, probably the most synthetic system ever built.

Chrysippus, he suggests, may have seen these tensions most clearly of all, and sharpened them so far that opponents took their weapons from his own books: Cicero reports that Chrysippus "armed" Carneades. Bréhier credits him with founding Stoicism a second time, by defending it against dissidents inside the school such as Ariston and against critics outside it, while conceding that this is what the scanty remains of his writings, and the authority later Stoics gave him, allow us to glimpse.

## The life of Chrysippus: origins, discipleship and the Academy (pp. 7–11)

Following Apollodorus as reported by Diogenes Laertius, Bréhier dates Chrysippus's birth to about 277 BC, his succession to Cleanthes as head of the school to 232, and his death to about 204. He was born at Soli on Cyprus to parents who had moved there from Tarsus in Cilicia, a region fought over by the Ptolemies, Demetrius and Antigonus. Bréhier finds it fitting that the philosopher whose moral ideal was cosmopolitan was born where local traditions had been crushed and constant changes of ruler made loyalty to one homeland impossible.

He is sceptical of the colourful stories. He rejects the report that Chrysippus had been a long-distance runner, since such runners were trained professionals and the story clashes with accounts of his slight build. He doubts, while finding it plausible, Hecaton's story that Chrysippus turned to philosophy after the royal treasury confiscated his fortune, because it is a stock edifying motif in philosophers' lives. He sets aside Diocles's claim that Chrysippus heard Zeno, on chronological grounds.

Chrysippus was a long-standing, more or less faithful pupil of Cleanthes and was about fifty when Cleanthes died. Anecdotes show him parading his dialectical skill and wounding his teacher's pride, and saying he needed only the doctrines, since he would find the proofs himself. Bréhier reads this as a pupil's pride in completing the teaching, not a disagreement of substance. Any break with Cleanthes, he argues, came no earlier than 247, because Chrysippus was still close enough to be offered, and to refuse, Cleanthes's place on an embassy to Ptolemy Euergetes.

Sotion's story that Chrysippus finally went over to the Academy of Arcesilaus and Lacydes Bréhier treats as a later guess invented to explain why Academic arguments were set out at such length in his books. Cicero says one of those books collected the Academic case against the senses so thoroughly that Carneades needed nothing else. What the tradition really preserves, Bréhier concludes, is Chrysippus's wish to know his opponents' arguments exactly and in full.

## The life of Chrysippus: Athens, withdrawal from politics and teaching (pp. 12–16)

After its defeat by Antigonus in 263, Athens never recovered political weight and became instead a university town and centre of cosmopolitan culture, as it remained until the end of pagan philosophy. The philosophical schools supplied kings with advisers and friends: Zeno and Arcesilaus were friends of Antigonus, Persaeus lived at his court, and Sphaerus took part in Cleomenes's attempt to restore the old Spartan constitution and later served Ptolemy Philopator. Anecdotes show how court life exposed philosophers to mockery and flattery.

Bréhier argues that Chrysippus drew his own conclusions from this and formed a different view of philosophy's social role. He refused to represent Stoicism at the Egyptian court and, against common practice, dedicated none of his treatises to a king. He confined himself to teaching and writing, serving the Stoic cause, as Athens itself now did, only through the indirect influence of education. Bréhier meets an objection in a note: Chrysippus did allow in *On Lives* that the sage may accept kingship or live at court, but the reason he gave was the sage's own profit, not the moral influence of his advice.

The sources agree on his devotion to the good order of his school: he wrote every day and kept regular hours among his pupils. He thought carefully about teaching, even advising that fees need not be demanded in advance but could wait on the pupil's progress. The central point of the chapter, on Bréhier's reading, is that his teaching was dogmatic and speculative at once. He rejected both the sophistic tradition of formal, practical training in arguing either side of a question, into which the Peripatetics had drifted, and the Academic method, revived from Gorgias, of arguing against every thesis. Plutarch reports his objection: if the goal is moral knowledge rather than a practical knack, such exercises are harmful, because the plausibility of a false thesis can carry the mind off and loosen its grip on the truth. He did not forbid setting out opposing arguments, but required that they be set out in a way that destroyed their persuasiveness.

## What survives of the 705 treatises, and the catalogue of Diogenes Laertius (pp. 17–22)

Chrysippus wrote 705 treatises. What survives is an incomplete list of titles in Diogenes Laertius, short fragments, only some tied to a named work, and a few papyri. Bréhier works only from what can safely be attributed to him and excludes the passages of Cicero and others in which modern scholars have claimed to detect Chrysippus as the model, attributions he calls hypothetical at best.

Several things explain the losses. Stoicism kept changing, so it never had the commentators who preserved and explained Plato and Aristotle. Most literal fragments come from opponents, Plutarch and Galen, who quote in order to refute. Chrysippus had few readers in the Hellenistic and Roman periods: Galen boasts that reading his logic was unusual, and Plutarch seems to work from extracts. His style was obscure and diffuse, broken by etymologies and quotations from poets that opponents mocked; by Epictetus's time his books were school texts whose meaning pupils disputed, and from Cicero on readers preferred a broader, popular manner to his dry, skeletal arguments.

The catalogue in Diogenes lists 119 logical works in five groups and 43 ethical works in three, and breaks off in the middle of a title. Bréhier, following von Arnim, argues that it cannot show how Chrysippus organised his thought. It is incomplete; its order conflicts with Chrysippus's own reported division of logic into signifiers and things signified, since the groups on things signified come before the group on signifiers that should logically precede them; and it contains no treatise on the criterion of truth, though Cicero makes the criterion one of the five parts of Chrysippus's logic. Many logical works have also strayed into the ethical list. Bréhier notes as characteristic that logical treatises are kept apart from works on the theory of knowledge, and concludes that the index was compiled, or at least altered, by a librarian ignorant of the most basic Stoic notions.

## The logical writings and the papyri (pp. 22–30)

The named fragments of the logical works are too thin to reconstruct any of them. Bréhier argues, however, that Aristotle's *Organon* often served Chrysippus as a template, whether he followed Aristotle or contradicted him. The *First Introduction to Syllogisms* opened with the indemonstrable forms of the Stoic syllogism, much as *Prior Analytics* proceeds. On opposition Chrysippus distinguished contraries, contradictories and privations as Aristotle did, even where he objected to Aristotle's rule that change runs only from possession to privation. His discussion of the possible and his argument for fate both start from Aristotle's theses and Aristotle's terms.

Bréhier sets aside, with von Arnim, one papyrus as a schoolboy exercise and examines two others. A fragment of the *Logical Questions* argues that if propositions about the past were themselves "past", they would need further propositions to express them, without end; the same holds of plurals and passives. Bréhier reads this as part of a wider argument that the *lekton*, the thing said, does not share the properties of the real events it expresses. The text then turns to impressions of thought that may be true or false, and to ambiguities in language: subject and predicate that cannot be told apart, words that may be singular or plural, ambiguous commands, epithets whose sense changes with what they qualify. Chrysippus admits such errors reach even the sage, but insists they are only apparent: the object of thought is not ambiguous, only the wording. Bréhier finds in the whole fragment one aim, to separate what belongs to a thought's verbal expression from what belongs to the thought. He judges that it shows the fault Aristotle's commentators blamed in Chrysippus, cases and examples in place of demonstration, and also a truly remarkable effort to distinguish the properties of language from those of thought.

A second papyrus, restored by von Arnim and attributed to Chrysippus by Keil, treats the sage's dialectical virtues as virtues: not assenting before cognition, not opining, being impossible to refute or to argue out of the truth.

## The physical writings: *Physics*, *On Nature* and *On the Gods* (pp. 30–40)

No physical titles appear in Diogenes's catalogue; later authors cite nineteen, and the total must have run to hundreds. Galen reports that Chrysippus often handled the same subject in several treatises without changing his views, which the fragments confirm. The excerpts reflect the interests of those who made them, above all Plutarch and Cicero, whose age cared more for the resulting picture of the world and its religious meaning than for the principles of physics. So the works on principles survive almost only as titles, while *On Providence*, *On the Gods*, *On Fate* and *On Nature* are comparatively well known. Bréhier argues that this bias has distorted interpretation, encouraging readers to reduce Stoic physics to a cosmology describing how the creative fire evolves.

The *Physics*, known through doxographical reports, opened with the names of Zeus and a cosmogony and destruction of the world closely following Zeno, went on to voice, seed, vision and the criterion, and in its third book set out Chrysippus's own theory of total mixture. *On Nature*, quoted literally by Plutarch, contained cosmogonic fragments, a vision of the world in eternal motion in which even disease and vice happen according to nature's reason, the world as an intelligent society of gods and men, and a treatment of providence that faced awkward cases such as bedbugs and the peacock's tail. Bréhier finds it remarkable that these physical treatises opened with a history of the world in the manner of the old Hesiodic and Orphic poems rather than with abstract principles.

*On the Gods* is known through Cicero and Philodemus. Book I set out the physical interpretation of each god, a kind of lexicon; Bréhier trusts Philodemus over Cicero here, since Cicero's Epicurean speaker had reason to isolate the abstract Stoic gods for ridicule. Book II fitted the poets' myths, from Orpheus and Musaeus to Hesiod, Homer and Euripides, to those interpretations. Book III returned to Zeus as the law and reason of nature, the basis of so-called Stoic monotheism. Bréhier calls the whole a theological encyclopedia joining popular and philosophical theology, of which Cornutus's *Compendium* is probably our least inaccurate image.

## The physical writings: *On Fate*, *On Providence* and *On the Soul* (pp. 40–48)

*On Fate* can be reconstructed fairly fully from the fragments that the philosopher Diogenianus quotes in order to attack them, preserved by Eusebius, together with material in Cicero, Plutarch and Alexander of Aphrodisias. Bréhier judges Diogenianus a late but independent witness. Book I defined fate and proved its existence, mixing arguments from authority (divination, the poets, the etymologies of the names of the Fates, common notions) with dialectical proofs: nothing happens without a cause, all things share a common breath and sympathy, and every proposition is true or false. Book II answered objections, above all that fate abolishes free will, by distinguishing principal causes from auxiliary or concurrent ones. Bréhier finds here, with remarkable clarity, the union of dialectic and authority that he regards as characteristic of Chrysippus.

*On Providence* survives in scattered lines, a damaged Herculaneum papyrus of Book II and two fragments of Book IV on the problem of evil. Bréhier holds it "not doubtful" that Book I was Cicero's model for the providence section at the end of *On the Nature of the Gods* II, since every fragment fits Cicero's account. As in *On Fate*, Chrysippus composed in the reverse order to Aristotle, stating the school's doctrine first and its difficulties afterwards, and Bréhier sees in this the same dogmatic cast as his teaching.

*On the Soul* is known through Galen, from whom von Arnim reconstructed much of Book I. Its second half argued that the ruling part of the soul (*hegemonikon*) is in the heart. The core premise, accepted without proof, was that if the passions are in the heart the reasoning part is there too; the supporting arguments came from feeling (we feel anger in the chest), poets, ordinary speech and gesture, etymology, and speech rising from the chest. Only afterwards did Chrysippus answer objections, including the medical fact that nerves start from the brain and the myth of Athena born from Zeus's head. In a rare agreement with Galen, Bréhier judges that this half deserves Galen's criticism for replacing demonstration with popular and rhetorical premises.

## The ethical writings: *On the Passions*, *On Lives*, *On Justice* (pp. 48–55)

*On the Passions* is known through long extracts in Galen. It had four books: three logical inquiries into what the passions are, and a fourth, called ethical or therapeutic, on their cure. Book I explained the received definitions of passion as an irrational motion contrary to nature and as an excessive impulse, illustrated by a runner who cannot stop. It then asked whether passions are judgments or phenomena that accompany judgments, Galen reporting the latter as Zeno's view. Galen complains that Chrysippus discussed only the meaning of the intellectualist theory and refused to examine Plato's. The book defined the four generic passions, three as opinions and desire as an impulse; Bréhier thinks these definitions were fairly new and that Galen's charge of contradiction is probably mistaken. Book II faced a difficulty for the theory that passions are judgments: grief fades with time though the opinion stays. Chrysippus answered that what time changes is not the judgment but the contraction of soul that first accompanied it, so, Bréhier infers, such bodily changes are elements of the passion but not its essence. Book IV pressed the analogy with medicine far: there must be a physician of the soul as there is of the body, and he should treat the patient without first correcting the patient's philosophical beliefs.

*On Lives* judged the ways of life in relation to wisdom and found none incompatible with it: kingship, court life, politics and commerce are admitted on condition the sage profits from them. It criticised the Peripatetic and Epicurean view that leisure belongs to the philosopher from the start, and gave rules for running a school, including the order in which subjects should be taught. *On the Republic* concerned the sage's conduct in the city; against Epicurus it denied that city life aims at pleasure, and it admitted the community of wives and even the legitimacy of incest.

The works on justice treated divine as well as human justice, argued against Epicurus that justice vanishes if chosen for pleasure, mocked Plato's use of fear of divine punishment, and argued from the simplicity of the soul against Plato's justice within the individual. Bréhier notes that Chrysippus often proves a Platonic thesis in his own way.

## Dialectic: why the Stoics made demonstration a virtue of the sage (pp. 59–65)

Book II opens with logic. Ancient and modern judgment agrees that Chrysippus's dialectical work was his most important achievement, and Bréhier reports, without endorsing it here, the view of Zeller and Stein that he was an organiser and defender of the system rather than a founder. Stoic dialectic, Bréhier holds, was Chrysippus's creation: Zeno had dealt mainly with the criterion and with solving sophisms, Cleanthes with little beyond fate.

The puzzle Bréhier poses is how a morality that takes its rules from nature, and so from the individual's spontaneous inclinations, could need science at all. The Cynics drew the conclusion that it does not. The Stoic answer is that only demonstration gives certainty, and spontaneity is an unreliable guide until tested by proof; without dialectic, Diogenes Laertius reports, the sage could not be unassailable in argument. Bréhier argues that this is why the Stoics made science an essential virtue of the sage, joining Cynic spontaneous morality to scientific reflection.

Aristotle had separated demonstrative logic, which proves properties from essences and alone has scientific value, from dialectic, the art of leading an interlocutor through questions from his own opinions. The Stoics turned the whole of logic into dialectic. Bréhier explains this by their being teachers before they were scientists: their aim was an unshakeable conviction in the pupil, and they joined the objective conditions of persuasion to strong subjective conviction. The dialectician does not discover new theses; he tests those that come naturally to the human mind, the existence of the gods or the reality of divination, until wavering opinions become firm beliefs proof against any counter-argument. Dialectic is therefore also a defensive weapon, which is why it contains a theory of sophisms. But unlike Aristotle's it is no longer the science of the probable. It is defined as the science of what is true, false and neither: the art of demonstrating, not of persuading. The Stoics thus absorbed demonstrative logic into dialectic and turned a practical art into a scientific discipline.

## Common notions: where spontaneity and science meet (pp. 65–68)

If dialectic starts from human opinions, how can it reach anything beyond opinion? The Stoic answer, Bréhier explains, is the theory of common or natural notions (*koinai ennoiai*, *prolepseis*). Every rational being has certain natural opinions, which together make up reason itself: in ethics the notion of the good and the just, in physics the existence of the gods and the survival of the soul. Argument that appeals to these notions stands on solid ground, and the Stoic always tries to bring his view back to one of them. The Stoics also used them to solve Plato's *Meno* problem of how inquiry is possible. Philodemus reports that dialectic achieves nothing unless it is joined to physics and ethics, which Bréhier explains by the need for common notions about physical and moral realities.

Bréhier argues that common notions are the point at which spontaneity and science coincide, and so dissolve the apparent conflict between moral spontaneity and scientific reflection. They are, in Seneca's phrase, the seeds of science rather than science itself.

They are not innate, despite a few texts that seem to say so. The Stoics were empiricists: every notion arises from sense impressions. A common notion forms either as a conclusion, as the gods are inferred from the beauty and order of the world, or by a kind of rational comparison, as the notion of the good is reached by comparing acts felt immediately to be good. What marks common notions off from other notions is that they arise early, before all others, and spontaneously, without art; reason is complete at about fourteen.

Here Bréhier argues against Stein. Stein took the notion of the good reached by rational comparison to be reflective, philosophical knowledge opposed to spontaneous common notions, and treated common notions as a limit the Stoics set on their empiricism, introduced to ground God and morality on something firmer than experience. Bréhier replies that comparison does not make a notion artificial, because every preconception involves a spontaneous reasoning; Stein's error comes from overlooking that reasoning. Given Stoic naturalism and optimism, the natural origin of a common notion is itself its guarantee, since nature and truth are necessarily in harmony.

## The *lekton*, the verb and the sign: why Stoic logic is not inductive logic (pp. 68–77)

Because dialectic addresses a hearer, its object and limit is what can be said in a complete sentence, the *lekton*. Bréhier argues that for the Stoics thought, apart from sense impressions, cannot be separated from speech, whether uttered or inward. Since only individual bodies exist, a sentence cannot express relations between essences as Aristotle's propositions did. It expresses events, and the verb, taken as a unit rather than split into copula and predicate, is what expresses them. Events are not realities but results of the activity of bodies: they are "incorporeals". Truth therefore belongs to propositions, not things: "it is day" is true, not the day. All Stoic propositions are singular, with subjects more or less determinate ("this one", "someone", "Socrates").

A dialectical thesis concerns an event not presently perceived, the non-evident, such as the existence of the gods or a future illness. Demonstration links it to a perceived fact by one of the connections language provides, above all the conditional ("if this woman has milk, she has given birth"), whose true antecedent is a sign of the consequent.

Brochard had argued that this nominalist logic was a first sketch of modern inductive logic, with the conditional playing the part of a law of succession between facts. Bréhier accepts this only with reservations that amount to rejection. For the modern inductivist, events are the stuff of reality and are causally linked; for Chrysippus they are unreal and causally inert, since only bodies act. What makes a conditional valid for Chrysippus is that the contradictory of the consequent conflicts with the antecedent, a test taken over from Aristotle's theory of opposites. The consequent must in effect repeat the antecedent, and no experiment is needed to decide validity. This, Bréhier argues, is why the logic is sterile and why ancient and modern critics have judged it harshly.

In practice the Stoics often preferred fertile conditionals to valid ones, accepting the inferences of medicine and divination and justifying them by physics, through the chain of fate, rather than by dialectic. Bréhier sees an evident contradiction here, made possible by the ambiguity of *akolouthia*, which means both logical consequence and the real chain of causes, and concludes that dialectic alone was insufficient.

## Reasoning, the indemonstrables and the art of stratagem (pp. 77–80)

For a conditional to ground a demonstration, its antecedent must actually hold: "if it is day, it is light" proves nothing unless it is day. The nerve of Stoic demonstration is therefore sensory evidence, and once the conditional is granted, demonstration reduces to a question of fact. Logical inquiry becomes the search for conclusive argument forms. Whether an argument is true depends on whether the facts in its premises are real; whether it demonstrates depends on whether its conclusion is a fact not directly perceived.

Bréhier sets out the five indemonstrable forms. The first two are conditional: if A then B, and A, so B; if A then B, and not B, so not A. The first is primary; the second, he notes, was devised for debate, to show that one cannot hold a conclusion together with its opposite. The third and fourth are disjunctive: either A or B, and A, so not B; either A or B, and not A, so B. The fifth works from a negated conjunction: not both A and B, and A, so not B. Chrysippus sometimes preferred this last form, the denial that two things hold together, to the conditional, a preference that returns in his treatment of fate.

Since Socrates, Bréhier observes, the art of discussion had been central to philosophy, and each doctrine established itself by defending itself against the others. Dialectic in this sense is an art of stratagem that conceals its goal as long as possible, and he explains the long chain-arguments of the Stoics by this rather than by any principle of continuity: each head of the school added new, unexpected intermediate steps. The paradox of Stoicism, on his reading, is that it made this defensive art into a theory of proof and a privilege of wisdom, rather than an exercise open to anyone.

## Truth, the sophists and the passive impression: against Stein (pp. 80–86)

Plato held that truth is reached only through science, the possession of a few. The sophists replied that knowing truth is the commonest thing: Protagoras made man the measure and sensation the grasp of truth. Bréhier places Stoic epistemology, to a degree, in the sophists' line. For the Stoics too truth can be grasped immediately and spontaneously, and perceiving it is something shared by all, while science, the inability to be mistaken, belongs to the sage alone. A true judgment can exist on its own, and science is the sum of true judgments as a city is the sum of its citizens. Since the Stoics go from part to whole, each partial truth must carry its own guarantee, so the criterion must lie in the most spontaneous and least reflective part of knowledge.

The impression (*phantasia*) is the image of the real produced in the soul by an external object: for Zeno and Cleanthes an imprint like a seal's, for Chrysippus a qualitative "alteration" of the soul. Unlike Aristotle, who reserved the word for memory images, the Stoics used it for the image of the real identified with sensation.

Stein argued that the impression is not purely passive. Chrysippus describes it as an alteration transmitted by a tension of the psychic medium, as tension in air carries sound; since psychic tension is the soul's own activity, the external shock would only be the occasion for an inner activity that is the essence of the impression. Bréhier gives three replies. Tension in air is the effect of the sound's impact, so psychic tension can equally be the effect of the external shock. If tension were a thinking activity, there would be no way to tell a sensory impression from a rational one, or that activity from rational assent; Stein himself cannot say how they differ. And the common identification of tension with knowledge has distorted the reading of Chrysippus, who in fact treats tension as something that supervenes on the sciences and virtues rather than constituting them. The impression must be taken literally as a passive state, and the starting point of Stoic epistemology is, Bréhier concludes, clearly sophistic.

## The comprehensive impression is passive: against Ganter, Brochard, Stein and Hirzel (pp. 86–98)

The Stoics adopted the sophists' starting point but claimed to reach immutable truth: they were the first to try to build a science of the sensible as such. That required a way of telling appearances apart from within appearance itself. Impressions are true or false, and among true ones only the "comprehensive" impressions (*phantasia kataleptike*) carry the mark of their truth; they are the criterion, already for Zeno.

The key question is whether an impression is comprehensive because of features of the image itself or because of how reason handles it. Most interpreters read *kataleptike* actively, as the impression that grasps. Bréhier argues against each version. Ganter's texts from Sextus show only that a healthy soul is a condition of such impressions, or that the mind attends to distinguishing marks at or after the moment of impression, not that the impression itself is active. Brochard held that for Zeno an impression is comprehensive because we give it firm assent, so that belief constitutes evidence and the Stoics anticipate Renouvier. Bréhier finds this very attractive but without textual support: every source puts the criterion in the impression, not in assent, and on Brochard's reading the phrase "comprehensive impression" would lose its meaning. Stein made the comprehensive impression an intermediate act preparing assent, but Zeno's famous simile leaves no room for it: the open hand is impression, including comprehensive impression; the fingers drawn in are assent, the first act of the soul; the fist is comprehension; one hand gripping the fist is science. Hirzel made the comprehensive impression the object that is grasped, which would require perceiving one's own impression, a step no text mentions and a materialist theory cannot allow.

Bréhier's conclusion is that the comprehensive impression is not an act at all. Like any impression it is a wholly passive image. Zeno's definition says it comes from what exists, matches it, and is stamped so that it could not come from what does not exist; each clause describes an effect of the object on the soul. Its comprehensiveness is immanent in the image and present before any activity of reason, as a particular clarity (*enargeia*). The word *kataleptike* names not the image's relation to its object but the act it infallibly provokes, comprehension. The way out of appearance is found within appearance and is as immediate as appearance itself.

## Comprehension, assent and the other criteria (pp. 99–108)

Comprehension (*katalepsis*) is the act of perception, distinct from the impression. It is an act of reason, assent given to an impression that is comprehensive, and assent is above all judgment. It is not the conclusion of a reasoning: the image's fidelity is seen immediately and compels assent, as weights pull down a scale. Reason here is spontaneous, not reflective. Only in the Middle Stoa, under pressure from Carneades, did comprehension become the product of reasoned criticism. Comprehension is also immediate perception of things: in the impression the object marks the soul, in comprehension the soul grips the object. Sensation in the full sense is comprehension of external things, which explains the report that all sensations are true while impressions may be false. Bréhier calls this distinction between sensation and impression the central point of Stoic epistemology, the one that separates it from the sensualism that came out of the sophists.

Diogenes Laertius reports other criteria: right reason, attributed to "older Stoics", whom Bréhier identifies with Zeno and Cleanthes (following Hirzel against Zeller); sensation and preconception, for Chrysippus; and for Boethus intellect, sensation, desire and science. Bréhier argues that all of these belong to the active phase of knowledge, assent and comprehension. Preconception (*prolepsis*) is rational comprehension, and its object is not a bare concept such as "god", which affirms nothing, but a proposition such as "the gods exist", the conclusion of a spontaneous reasoning all people make before any training. It is, in other words, a common notion.

Does bringing in reason as judge contradict placing truth in an image prior to reason? Bréhier adopts Bonhöffer's solution: "criterion" has two senses, the distinguishing mark of truth (the comprehensive impression) and the faculties that use that mark (sensation and reason). Against Stein, he holds that Chrysippus's preconception is not simply the older Stoics' right reason, because it is common to all, whereas right reason is virtue and belongs to the sage. His conclusion is that Chrysippus limited the criterion to reason's spontaneous faculties, perhaps being the first to distinguish the artless reason common to all from the reason trained by dialectic that belongs to the sage.

## Physics: bodies, individuals and the "peculiarly qualified" (pp. 109–116)

The Stoics admit only bodies, and each body is an individual differing from every other in essence and qualities. Bréhier stresses that they looked for the reason an individual exists, and has the nature it has, inside the body itself, rejecting both Plato's participation in Ideas and Aristotle's gradual realisation of an undetermined matter. Events, on this view, are incorporeal effects playing on the surface of a being, adding nothing to it; Bréhier credits Chrysippus with first drawing the extreme consequences. Against the objection that constant change shows sensible things to be unreal, Chrysippus answered that motion is an act, not a potency on its way to realisation, and that in each being it takes the form of a tension (*tonos*) running from centre to periphery and back.

Qualities too are bodies, since whatever acts is a body. Bréhier argues that the real motive was to make the individual independent of its passing qualities and so secure its permanence. The danger, pressed by Plotinus and Galen, is that a being stripped of its qualities leaves only an undetermined substrate. Chrysippus's answer was the "peculiarly qualified" individual (*idios poion*), a term Bréhier finds first in him: an irreducible originality that cannot be explained by combining pre-existing principles, arriving all at once, departing all at once, and remaining the same throughout the life of the compound. Individuality is positive, not a falling short of the species.

Chrysippus's puzzle about two substances that come to share one peculiar quality, of which the first perishes, rests on a principle of the identity of indiscernibles: no two individuals can be exactly alike. The sources conflict on whether one being can carry two peculiar qualities: Plutarch says yes, Philo quotes Chrysippus saying no. Bréhier resolves the conflict through the case of Zeus and providence at the conflagration. Chrysippus certainly held that both survive, and to keep that he gave up the ban on two peculiar qualities in one being; Plutarch's version is the final Stoic view. One individual can thus contain another as a part, as a leaf's individuality is part of a tree's. Bréhier ties this discontinuous world of individuals to Stoic epistemology, in which knowledge is made of distinct acts of contact with objects.

## Dualism: the active and passive principles, pneuma and tension (pp. 116–125)

From another side the Stoics are thoroughly dualist. Every being comes from two principles, an active one that makes it one and a passive one, inert matter that takes any form and keeps the same total quantity. The pair is borrowed from Aristotle but read differently: in Aristotle agent and patient are separate individuals and action passes from one to the other, while in the Stoics they are inseparable principles in the making of a single being. And where Aristotle needed a third, moving cause to bring form into matter, the Stoic form is itself the shaping force.

Bréhier argues that the Old Stoa never had a single theory of principles, only a general scheme realised in three separate and perhaps irreconcilable theories. First, across the universe, God acts on matter, which has no quality of its own and becomes the four elements under divine action; since both act or are acted on, both are bodies, and the cosmos they produce is one individual. Second, within each element a quality-less substrate is paired with a defining quality, itself a body; and between elements, fire and air are active and "contain", while earth and water are passive and "are contained", a cruder version of the Peripatetic and medical division of active and passive qualities. Third, the unity of any body comes from an internal force, *pneuma*, a mixture of fire and air, a warm breath.

Bréhier traces *pneuma* to a crude biology: doctors took the warm breath of living things to be the life principle, ranked by its proportion of heat. The Stoics extended it to every body, from stone to rational being, so that each thing has its own breath and every quality is an agent. The manner of acting came from ethics: *tonos* was the moral energy of Cynic ethics, applied to physics by Cleanthes but given its full meaning only by Chrysippus, bound up with total mixture. *Tonos* holds a being's parts together, with an outward movement producing qualities and an inward one producing unity.

The resulting picture contains contradictions: the active principle is sometimes one god and sometimes many breaths, the passive sometimes bare substrate and sometimes earth and water, and body is said to come from two principles that are themselves bodies.

## Not materialism: total mixture, cause and the categories (pp. 125–135)

Bréhier argues that the Stoic corporeal agent is so peculiar that "materialism" hardly fits. It is purely active and suffers nothing, which for Aristotle is the mark of immaterial agents; and though it mixes with what it acts on, it remains wholly unaltered.

Hence total mixture, which Bréhier credits Chrysippus with developing. Where Aristotle saw one kind of mixture, Chrysippus distinguished fusion, in which both substances are altered, from interpenetration, in which each keeps its properties throughout, as incense in air or fire in red-hot iron; a single drop of wine thrown into the sea spreads through all of it. A breath that acts on a body without being affected must penetrate it while remaining itself. Bréhier reads total mixture as an answer to Plato's old problem of participation: how can the formal element be present in sensible things without being altered? Here the form is a material breath, but like the Idea it must stay pure, an impassive activity working without wear. Stoic "pneumatism" therefore runs in the direction opposite to what is usually called materialism.

On causes, only what acts is a cause; passive matter is a condition, not a cause, and unlike Aristotle's matter it contributes nothing to change. Motion is an act complete in itself, renewed at each instant. Bréhier criticises the confusion of motion with activity, which kept the ancients from a scientific dynamics: the most mobile body, breath, becomes the cause par excellence, so that against Aristotle the first cause moves. Causes are coordinated, not ranked, and every cause is treated as if it were first. Cause and effect are of different kinds: the cause is a body, the effect an incorporeal event expressible by a verb, so nothing of the cause passes into its effect.

The categories belong to physics, not logic, which shows once more how cut off dialectic is from the real. Substrate and quality are both bodies and together roughly equal Aristotle's substance; "disposed" and "relatively disposed" are external aspects, not accidents that make the individual. The categories overlap, and Bréhier reads them as points of view on the real rather than properties that add up to a concrete being.

## The elements and their transmutation (pp. 135–143)

For the Stoics, as for Aristotle, an element is less a material than an active force, and elements turn into one another. But Aristotle treated elemental forces as mere tools of form, likening the view that makes them final principles to crediting a saw with the finished product; the Stoics held exactly that view.

Two theories of transmutation were available. Heraclitus's was physical: the elements arise in fixed order from fire by condensation (fire, air, water, earth) and return by rarefaction. Aristotle's was logical: four qualities in opposed pairs (hot and cold, dry and wet) combine to make four elements, and change occurs when one quality is overcome by its opposite. Bréhier argues that the simpler Heraclitean theory dominates Stoic thought, and that Aristotle's influence appears only with Chrysippus, who studied the logic of opposites closely. Chrysippus gave each element a single quality (fire hot, air cold, earth dry, water wet), so that the elements themselves, not their component qualities, are opposed. This is the root of his dispute with Aristotle over air: if fire is the hot and air arises from fire, air must be its opposite, the cold.

Aristotle's theory did not take hold because it implied that change runs in a circle through all the elements indefinitely, so that no element is more elementary than another. In the Stoic theory change starts from fire and can return to fire only by retracing its steps, which makes fire the element par excellence and gives change a rhythm of periods with beginning and end. The downward change is spontaneous: fire gives rise to air by putting itself out. The upward change is not: a lower element becomes fire only when fire assimilates it, as fire devours fuel, the stars feed on vapours from sea and land, and the life-breath feeds on exhalations of the blood.

This looks like a monism of self-transforming fire, but the pull to dualism is strong: the Stoics often treat all four elements as the matter of beings, held together by *pneuma* set apart to govern them. Bréhier finds in this materialism more than the seeds of spiritualism, so that Posidonius's later Platonising should not be seen as a catastrophe. His method, stated here, is not to remove the Stoics' contradictions at any cost but to explain them: they took in every thesis compatible with their general direction without checking whether those theses agreed with one another.

## Cosmology: Heraclitus disfigured, the demiurge fire and a world that is not a god (pp. 143–150)

The framework of Stoic cosmology is Heraclitus's, but Bréhier argues that the Stoics' optimism, belief in providence and religious attitude to the cosmos disfigure Heracliteanism completely. Zeno's arguments that the world began and will end, borrowed largely from earlier physicists, depend on a thesis Aristotle rejected, the sympathy of the world's parts.

Like a living thing, the world is born from a seed animated by breath. Primal fire, putting itself out, produced air and through air the liquid element, the seed; from it earth condensed at the centre and air and ether rose outward. The letter is Heraclitean, but the instability and chance that gave Heraclitus his sublimity are gone: Chrysippus feels at home in a world made on purpose for gods and men, and his world gives an impression of stability rather than flux.

The primal fire is, in one sense of "cosmos", the world itself: the unborn, incorruptible god who persists through every world-order and takes the world back into himself. It is pure mind, and in the conflagration the world becomes wholly soul. Unlike Heraclitus's fire, it is an individual that remains identical with itself while fiery substances change; Chrysippus, Bréhier remarks, uses a monist's images and speaks as a dualist. In forming the world this god acts as a demiurge. Against Barth and others who read Stoicism as evolutionist, Bréhier stresses that gradual development is wholly absent: the world is perfect from the start, with every species present and the useful arts invented at once. He calls this the first sketch of an anti-Heraclitean idea, the idea of creation.

The generated world, moreover, is not itself a god. The primal fire is one individual and pure thought; the world is a system of beings, an animal of soul and body in which divinity lies only in the soul. Later Stoics such as Seneca called the world divine, but that pantheism, Bréhier argues, is not Chrysippus's thesis; he answers the passages in Cicero and Plutarch that seem to say otherwise. The picture of the world as a self-sufficient animal feeding on itself comes from Plato's *Timaeus*. Chrysippus placed the world's ruling reason in the heavens, in the ether or the sphere of the fixed stars, where Cleanthes had placed it in the sun.

## The void, sympathy and individuation: why the Old Stoa was not pantheist (pp. 150–155)

On the arrangement of the world the Stoics follow Aristotle closely: a motionless spherical earth at the centre, water around it, then air, the ethereal spheres of the planets and the sphere of the fixed stars. Two differences matter. The world sits in an infinite void, which leaves it free to expand in the conflagration; its limit comes from the internal force holding its parts together, not from anything surrounding it. And the parts of the world share a sympathy, a coordination and "breathing together" that replaces the hierarchy of Aristotle's cosmos: there is no void within the world, and exchange runs constantly between earth and heaven, the sun fed by exhalations of the sea and the moon by river water.

This leaves a problem of individuation. We can see how the primal fire makes the world-animal, by giving itself a body. We cannot see how either the fire or the world-animal relates to the many individual beings, and the sources give no answer.

A pantheist answer is often attributed to the Stoics: each being's breath is a fragment of the world-soul. Such statements exist in the Middle and later Stoa: Posidonius makes the human soul a fragment of the ether, Seneca makes us members of a divine world. Bréhier affirms with certainty that this is not Old Stoic doctrine. Chrysippus describes the origin of the soul differently; he denies that the world is a god; and the peculiar quality of an individual is indivisible, arising and vanishing all at once, so it cannot be a portion of something larger.

What fits Chrysippus instead is another of Zeno's images: the world stands to the individual as parent to offspring. The individual is not a part of the whole but a being of the same nature as the perfect being, only imperfect, as Cicero puts it, as a puppy is to a dog. This gives the individual far more independence. Joined to the sudden emergence of individual quality, it shows Chrysippus coming close, for the second time, to the idea of creation.

## The conflagration and eternal return (pp. 156–158)

The world's destruction reverses its birth: a universal conflagration returns all the elements to the primal fire, an expansion into the void after the contraction of the world-order. Bréhier notes that *ekpyrosis* means the completed change into fire rather than the burning itself. He argues that the Stoics were not catastrophists but accepted slow processes: the gradual drawing-up of earth and water by the heavenly fire, which begins with the ordering of the world itself, is already part of the final change. Dio Chrysostom speaks of the elements wearing away over a time that seems infinite to us.

The conflagration is read optimistically, as renewal rather than death. Chrysippus says it is not the death of the world but its universal life: everything becomes soul, a divinisation and a victory of Zeus over all things.

The cycle of birth and destruction repeats for ever, the rhythm of the life of the universal god. The law of development is the same in every period, and since everything is linked down to the smallest detail, each period repeats the last exactly. Chrysippus said it is not impossible that after our death and long ages we will be restored in our present form; Nemesius reports that there will again be a Socrates and a Plato, with the same friends and fellow citizens, not once but endlessly. Bréhier contrasts this Zeus, a constant and firm will, with Heraclitus's god at play. For him the Stoic eternal return expresses the rigour and constancy of the divine will and is a guarantee against instability: the opposite of Nietzsche's eternal return, which means chance, purposelessness and freedom from the servitude of ends.

## The soul: a body, its development and its unity (pp. 158–168)

By the Stoics' time "soul" meant three things: the life-force, the subject that endures through a being's destiny, and the subject of knowledge and passion. The Stoics start from the first, the soul as life-breath, and try to account for the other two. Chrysippus's syllogism, reported by Chalcidius, runs: we breathe and live by the same thing; we breathe by natural breath; so we live by it; we live by the soul; so the soul is natural breath. Cleanthes and Chrysippus argued that the soul is a body from the bodily expression of emotion, the moral likeness of children to parents, sense-knowledge and, Chrysippus's addition, death. Bréhier stresses that this is closer to very old ideas than to modern materialism: the mind is not a product of matter but an agent, and non-thinking breaths such as the growth-force of plants are degradations of soul-breath, not its source.

The seed carries a breath that is a fragment of the parent's soul and contains seminal reasons, the fated law of its development; heredity follows whichever ancestral reason prevails. The embryo's breath is only *physis*, a plant-like nature; at birth cold air tempers it into *psyche*, able to receive impressions and respond with impulse; reason comes with the common notions around seven, maturity at fourteen. Degrees of tension explain sleep, old age and the passions, and Chrysippus's doctrine founded the "pneumatic" medical school.

Against Plato, the Stoics put inclination and knowledge in one seat, since every impression presents something to pursue or avoid. Against Aristotle, no separate nutritive soul is needed: the rational soul unites knowledge, impulse and vital force. The eight parts of the soul, the five senses, speech, the generative part and the ruling part, are emanations of the ruling part (*hegemonikon*) in the heart, currents of breath sent through the organs. Chrysippus, unlike Cleanthes, held that the substance of the ruling part itself extends through the body. Bréhier endorses this as more Stoic: the ruling part is in the body as providence is in the world. He rejects Ganter's and Stein's reading of a two-level soul, an unconscious organic soul with a conscious ruling part within it, on three grounds, the chief being that soul as distinct from nature already includes impression, a conscious function.

## Assent and the "pessimistic" conception of reason; the fate of the soul (pp. 168–172)

Faculties are not parts. The faculties of the ruling part are impression, impulse, assent (*synkatathesis*) and reason. Bréhier finds the great originality of the Stoics in joining knowing to acting: not, as moderns would say, that every sensation brings a reaction, but that every action presupposes an impression, following the chain sensation, impression, assent, impulse. In a rational being impression and impulse come under judgment: we assent only to impressions judged true and yield only to impulses judged to accord with nature, so that impression becomes perception and impulse becomes considered will. Plutarch reports Chrysippus's view that acts following an impression without assent are fictions: in a rational being every act is rational.

From this Bréhier draws what he calls the pessimistic Stoic conception of reason. Reason makes us masters of our thoughts and acts, and so opens the way equally to virtue and to vice, truth and error. Reason is common to all; wisdom belongs to few. For Plato, reason is simply good and morality is reason ruling blind impulse, so the good lies in a sense outside the individual. For the Stoics the spontaneous act is neither good nor bad, and the rational act is not good in itself, because it carries the possibility of going wrong. Evil is therefore inside the soul, and the moral struggle takes place within the very substance of reason. Bréhier makes this the basis of Stoic moral activity and returns to it in the theodicy.

The Stoics cared little about the soul's fate after death and reacted against popular and mystical beliefs in rewards and punishments; Chrysippus mocked the popular pictures of punishment. He fitted survival into his physics: the breath that held a body together can hold itself together, and souls become spherical and wander near the earth, feeding on its exhalations. Zeno had them last a long time, Cleanthes until the conflagration; Chrysippus held that only the souls of the wise survive. Bréhier judges the doctrine loosely held and without role in the ethics, for which a single instant of happiness is worth an eternity.

## Fate as a religious reaction: Heraclitus and the unity of the laws of nature (pp. 172–182)

Chrysippus called fate by many names: reason, truth, cause, nature, necessity. Bréhier sets it against its background. Greek thinkers tended to explain human destinies and cosmic events by different kinds of cause: Plato explained physics by intelligible principles but turned to myth for human destiny, where fate is a moral law of justice working with impersonal rigour. When faith in justice weakened, fate became Tyche, the arbitrary chance that hands out roles like a playwright. Meanwhile Plato and Aristotle made cosmic causality rational, which should have made a special principle for human lots unnecessary.

Bréhier reads Stoicism as a religious reaction. It brought back a fate governing each person's lot, extended it to every being, and, crucially, made the principle that explains things scientifically the same as the religious principle that governs human life. Every philosophy was to be a religion and every religion a philosophy. This had a bad consequence, blocking positive scientific method more than Plato or Aristotle did, and a lasting one: the principle of the unity of the laws of nature, settling everything down to the smallest detail, where Aristotle had only ranked kinds of causality. Even events contrary to an individual's nature are in accord with nature as a whole.

Heraclitus anticipated much of this, but Bréhier distinguishes them. For Heraclitus the logos is central and fate a symbol of it; for the Stoics fate comes first and logos is one aspect of it. Heraclitus's logos is elusive and without providence; Stoic fate is a fixed principle of optimism and a ground for trusting the universe. One of Chrysippus's proofs of fate is that the wise are content with what happens, which they could only be if they felt secure. Where Heraclitus's logos triumphs over what it overpowers, the Stoic sage surrenders completely, with neither humiliation nor tragic joy.

Chrysippus claimed to absorb the philosophical notions of his predecessors into fate: Aristotle's nature, Plato's truth, Democritus's necessity, even chance, whose obscurity exists only for human thought. Fate is the reason by which events happen; it is truth, since what makes all events true must lie in it; it is nature, an eternal ordered motion; it is necessity. Where identification was impossible, as with Aristotle's spontaneity, Chrysippus simply denied that uncaused events exist.

## The proofs of fate: causality against Carneades, sympathy and bivalence (pp. 182–189)

Chrysippus wanted three accepted principles to lead of themselves to fate: causality, universal sympathy and the principle that every proposition is true or false.

Causality alone does not give fate. Fate also claims that causes are linked in one continuous, eternal chain. Carneades, reported by Cicero, accepted that voluntary movements have causes but denied that they have external antecedent causes, and so attacked the step from causality to fate. Chrysippus's link was the principle of antecedent causes: nothing moves without a cause, therefore everything happens through antecedent causes, therefore everything happens by fate. Plain causality asks only that an event be explained by the nature of its agent and patient; the principle of antecedent causes ties each event to an earlier one and so, step by step, to the whole universe. Bréhier calls this principle a close counterpart of the modern principle of causality and its statement one of Chrysippus's glories: the older picture of causes as independent actors entering the stage one by one denied the unity of the cosmos, and Chrysippus came close to determinism. But the argument has a gap: the unity of the causal series is assumed, not proved, since nothing shows that one series cannot be independent of another.

The argument from sympathy survives only through Cicero's criticism: all things act on one another, as climate affects health, character and so choices. Cicero granted the "contagion of things" but denied that it amounts to fate, and Chrysippus's bridging argument is lost.

The argument from bivalence must not be confused with Diodorus's. Diodorus went directly from "every proposition is true or false" to the necessity of future events. Chrysippus went through causality: bivalence requires that every event's place be fixed, which only its producing causes can do. Bréhier argues that what Chrysippus establishes is therefore not brute necessity but hypothetical necessity, or determinism. That is what let him escape the "lazy argument" Aristotle had used against Diodorus, that if the future is necessary deliberation is pointless. His reply was the doctrine of the co-fated (*confatalia*): an event is necessary only through the earlier events it involves, not in itself, so that recovery is fated together with calling the doctor.

## Fate and freedom: modalities, future contingents and the cylinder (pp. 189–196)

Two Stoic theses had to be reconciled: our assent and rational impulses are "in our power", and fate makes our acts depend on the world order. Chrysippus was the first Stoic to attempt this; Zeno and Cleanthes apparently saw no difficulty. His method was to deny that necessity follows from fate while showing that fate is compatible with self-mastery.

He could use neither Aristotle's reply to Diodorus, that only the disjunction of outcomes is necessary, nor Epicurus's, the swerve and the denial of causality. Stoic modalities apply to singular propositions: the necessary is what is true and can never become false, the possible what could pass from false to true. Past truths are necessary. Diodorus argued that in a true conditional with a past antecedent and a future consequent, the consequent inherits the antecedent's necessity. Chrysippus denied that this holds in every case. Yet he accepted the sign-reading of seers and doctors, in which a past fact is a sure sign of a fated future one. In what sense, then, were other outcomes possible? Bréhier cannot see that Chrysippus escaped except by what he calls dialectical juggling: holding that a true future proposition is not necessary because it will cease to be true once the day is past, and recasting the diviners' conditionals as negated conjunctions ("it is not the case both that he was born at the rising of the Dog Star and that he will die at sea"), denying joint truth in place of necessary consequence.

His second and central move was the distinction of causes, made, as Cicero says, to escape necessity while keeping fate. A cylinder rolls when pushed, but the push is only the auxiliary, antecedent cause; the principal and perfect cause is the cylinder's own shape. Fate says only that the principal cause produces nothing without the auxiliary one. Applied to assent: there is no assent without an impression, but the impression does not explain assent without the nature of the reason that gives it. Chrysippus sought, in Bréhier's phrase, to affirm both horns of the dilemma at once.

A modern determinist would object that the cylinder's roundness is itself an event with causes. Bréhier argues that the objection does not arise for Chrysippus, because his analysis stops at individual beings whose natures are not broken down into events: bodies remain the subjects and principles of their actions. Fate appears less as a chain of events than as the harmonious concert of agents, each acting according to its own nature. Cicero called Chrysippus an arbiter between the camps; the Cynic Oenomaus mocked the position as half-slavery.

## The gods: common notion, cosmic monotheism and allegory (pp. 196–205)

Knowledge of the gods is for the Stoics both religious, a mystery into which knowledge of the world gradually initiates us, and a common notion that reason draws from the sight of the world. The sophistic Enlightenment had explained the gods as poetic fictions, legislators' devices, deified heroes or names for useful things. Bréhier notes that the Stoics accepted all these as partial origins, but used Enlightenment critique to rescue religion rather than destroy it. The conventional gods were brought back to life by the natural notion of an ordering god inferred from the order and beauty of the cosmos. The key difference, as he sees it, is that the conventional gods are conceived in relation to people and cities, the natural god in relation to the cosmos, so that there is no relation of dependence between human beings and God.

Chrysippus's proof, in Cicero, assumes that the world is the work of a rational being; since man, the only rational being we know, could not have made its order, there must be a higher one. Usefulness also served as proof: Chrysippus argued from the fact that altars are built.

The result is what Bréhier calls a cosmic monotheism: God is defined by his function as the rational force that orders the world, and his personality is fixed only by the law of the world's unity. He contrasts the Hebrew monotheism of a fatherly will in direct contact with human wills. The bearer of the function is left vague: for Zeno artistic fire, for Cleanthes the world-soul or ether, for Chrysippus the world, heaven, nature, fate, the ruling part of the world-soul, fire and ether. Each candidate is chosen for its function, so all are the same many-named Zeus.

The one force appears many as it works through the world, and each point where it acts yields a god, which recovers the popular gods: Demeter is the earth or the breath in it. Bréhier finds the novelty not in treating gods as natural forces but in making them transformations of one force, perishable gods to which the one returns at the conflagration, a rational polytheism. Chrysippus applied allegory systematically to Orpheus, Hesiod and Homer, to keep a meaning for Greek civilisation. Bréhier argues that the centre of gravity of Stoic thought is a religious reaction against the rationalism of physics that makes physics argue for religion: the Stoics were the inventors of religious apologetics. Allegory is not an appendix; the images it keeps colour the concepts, so that the artistic fire seems personal because it is also Zeus.

## Providence, the problem of evil, and the relation of human beings to the gods (pp. 205–214)

Providence follows from God's nature as what sustains the world; it is not an added will that might have been absent. Bréhier notes a singular contrast: providence is in essence a force inside the universe, yet its action looks as external as an artist's, arranging plants for animals and animals for men. It is not yet tied to inner moral life, as it will be in Marcus Aurelius.

The theodicy, much of it Chrysippus's, had to reconcile purpose with a monism that, unlike Platonism, had no second principle to blame for evil. God cannot be the cause of shameful things, as law cannot cause lawlessness. Some arguments were borrowed: evil as punishment and example from Plato, evil as a test of virtue from the Cynics. Bréhier observes that they turn evil into good rather than explain it, and that the Stoic theodicy does not deny that evil is real, a denial he finds in Epictetus but not, against Capelle, in the Old Stoa. The reason is the Stoics' fundamental pessimism.

Chrysippus's own argument, in Gellius, is that some evils come about as by-products (*kata parakolouthesin*) of real goods: a thin skull, good for the head, brings fragility with it. Most interpreters read this as a dualism in which providence yields to a necessity above it. Bréhier grants that Cleanthes held such a dualism, since for him vicious acts were the only events not from the divine will. But for Chrysippus he denies it: Chrysippus explicitly identifies fate with providence, necessity and Zeus, so the evils are not an external necessity to which the divine will gives way; they are willed together with the whole. There is no dualism, only a casuistry of the creative will based on the priority of the whole over its parts. The "logical" argument that every good implies its contrary, which Barth rightly criticises as sophistical, rests on the pessimistic view of reason: vice is not a revolt of lower parts but a state of reason itself, born where virtue is born, and this simultaneous birth of opposites is at the base of moral life. The theodicy obliged Chrysippus to justify evils case by case, down to absurd detail.

The early Stoa pictured the relation of human beings to the gods on the model of a well-ordered city: common reason is the law uniting gods and men, and civic feeling, losing its object as the Greek cities declined, moved into religion. A more inward conception, reason as God's gift and God as father, appears in germ in Cleanthes's Hymn and flourishes only from the Middle Stoa.

## Ethics: the sage, holiness and the paradoxes (pp. 215–222)

Stoic ethics asks the question of all Greek ethics: how to be happy, how to escape the inward ills of error and grief and the outward ills of poverty, illness and insult. All the schools of the period look for the lever in the individual rather than, like Plato, in the reform of institutions, having learned that laws are conventional. The Stoics had a further reason: the cosmos already is a moral reality containing supreme wisdom. Reform comes from the head of a school acting privately as a director of souls, a mission Bréhier traces to the Cynics. The aim is an inward change that brings wisdom and happiness together, since only the bad man can be deceived, coerced or insulted.

Bréhier argues that interpreters have misread Stoic wisdom by projecting onto it a later distinction between holiness, a superhuman perfection, and ordinary uprightness, and then filing it under uprightness. That distinction did not yet exist, and the difficulties of Stoic ethics may have helped create it. The portrait of the sage combines both ideas. On the side of holiness: sages are a tiny separate class, perhaps none has ever existed; their happiness is perfect and, for Chrysippus, no less than divine; wisdom comes suddenly, by total transformation, sometimes unnoticed; the sage's will is one with the will that governs the universe; all who are not sages are equally bad and all faults are equal.

The paradoxes, which the Stoics admitted were paradoxes, give the sage every virtue and also every good a Greek counted as happiness: he alone is free, rich, beautiful, a king, a magistrate, a poet. Bréhier notes that this hands the sage even the Peripatetic goods of body and fortune. He sets out three readings: the Cynic one, symbolic revenge on the crowd; the view that using any good requires knowing how and when, which is wisdom; and the view that the sage alone has the dispositions to possess these things when the occasion arises. The last two tend toward common sense and miss what is specific to Chrysippus, for whom the sage's qualities imply one another in chains of argument, so that wisdom is indivisible and exists only whole. But the faultless sage was not a Stoic invention; it was the shared programme of all the schools since the Cynics, and Bréhier warns that it is vain to look for Stoic originality there.

## Moral naturalism, indifferents and the "preferred" (pp. 222–233)

The doctrine of the sage was exclusive and dangerous, since it put philosophy outside daily life. The Stoics' real originality, Bréhier argues, was to limit it so that it could coexist with a moral regulation of ordinary conduct, and Zeno's master stroke was to join the theory of wisdom to physics. Ariston left the school by denying that link, and for defending it against him Chrysippus could be called the second founder of the Porch. Stoic ethics reconciles the naturalism of Academics such as Polemo, Zeno's teacher, with the Cynic sage: the virtuous life is the life according to nature. Against Hirzel, Bréhier holds that Zeno's formula already included nature. What is new is that the relevant nature is universal nature, of which ours is a fragment, and that Chrysippus argued this natural good is identical with the honourable.

There were two opponents. Academics and Peripatetics said Zeno mutilated nature, since we are body as well as soul. Ariston, from the same principle, argued that if health and wealth accord with nature the will must pursue them, so virtue must be indifference to them; but that leads to a contingent will, ruinous to Stoic physics, and to a quietism that makes moral counsel impossible.

The notion of the good grows from common notions. Every animal seeks first not pleasure, against Epicurus, but what preserves it. In a rational animal impulse is lit by judgment, and by comparing natural ends the will reaches a general notion of the good. Bréhier stresses that in a rational being the will does not serve the tendency; it replaces it. Does reason then have its own end? Bréhier finds two answers hard to reconcile: reason generalises from natural acts, so virtue is the direction of the will toward that common good; or reason understands universal nature and brings its will into harmony with Zeus, as in Chrysippus's saying that if he knew he was fated to be ill he would have an impulse toward illness.

Hence the indifferents: health, wealth, life and death are neither good nor bad, but indifferent only to the human end; unlike having an odd number of hairs, they still attract or repel. This allowed a whole body of practical advice. Zeno measured their value by their distance from wisdom, an obscure standard; common Stoic doctrine measured it by conformity to the primary inclinations. Bréhier concludes that below the ethics of the sage there now stands a second, independent ethics with its own scale of goods, which Chrysippus even allowed to be called goods and evils.

## Appropriate acts and the two superposed moralities (pp. 233–238)

An appropriate act (*kathekon*) is one that accords with nature, whether done virtuously or not: seeking wealth, taking part in public life, raising children, returning a deposit. The sage's right action (*katorthoma*) is a perfect appropriate act. The early Stoics, Chrysippus especially, tried to derive appropriate acts from wisdom: to test an act, ask whether the sage would do it. Hence a literature of questions such as whether the sage will get drunk, and maxims of the form "the sage will, or will not". The sage has them all at once in his will; the ordinary person meets them one by one as rules from outside. They are, Bréhier says, the small change of wisdom.

For the sage they are not duties in our sense. Returning a deposit adds nothing to his virtue or happiness; the good lies in the manner of acting. He marries or enters politics only conditionally, if nothing prevents; the value of such acts is only probable, and circumstances may require abstaining. Hence Stoic casuistry. The decisive example is suicide. Whether to stay alive depends on whether, in the circumstances, it is more in accord with nature to live or to leave, which turns on having the objects of the primary inclinations such as health and sound senses, not on wisdom. Chrysippus held Plato wrong to urge death on those who lack the art of living: a bad man may properly remain alive if his natural needs are met, and a supremely happy sage ought to die if he is sick, poor and despised.

Bréhier draws the consequence. The measure of appropriate acts is a rule shared by sage and fool, conformity to nature as the sum of basic needs, and it founds a universal morality beneath the morality of the sage, one that could develop without it, as it did in the Middle Stoa, in treatises on duties such as Cicero's drawn from Panaetius, in which the sage has almost vanished. Wisdom is both the summit of an ascent rooted in natural inclinations and the starting point of a descent that prescribes conformity to nature; yet its scale of values cannot be compared with the fool's. The result is two superposed moralities, of right actions and of appropriate acts. The greatness of Stoicism, for Bréhier, is Zeno's effort, renewed by Chrysippus against Ariston, to hold them together: seeking the principle of duties above them against Academic naturalism, and, against Pyrrhonian quietism, making daily life serious by showing that duties exist in it.

## Virtue: Chrysippus's intellectualism against Cleanthes and Ariston (pp. 239–248)

Bréhier distinguishes ethics meant for education from ethics meant only to define morality, and finds in the educational aim one root of Stoic intellectualism, strongest in Chrysippus. Zeno reduced all virtues to prudence (*phronesis*), which he identified with knowledge, merging what Aristotle had kept apart. Cleanthes let the Cynic side win: virtue is tension (*tonos*), called strength when strong enough and continence when it perseveres, and knowledge drops out. Chrysippus returned to an extreme intellectualism.

Virtue is acquired, not innate: nature gives everyone upright inclinations, so all begin innocent, and virtue is gained by intellectual means. Against Cleanthes, Chrysippus allowed non-intellectual capacities such as strength and health of soul, which depend on the tension of the ruling part, but only as accompanying powers, not as the fundamental virtues. It follows that whatever affects the intellect affects virtue, so virtue can be lost through drunkenness or melancholy. Vice has two sources: things themselves, as when the infant, protected from pain, confuses pleasure with the good; and the noise of popular opinion, beginning with mothers and nurses. Hence Chrysippus's insistence on education from the cradle.

On the unity of virtue, Zeno and Cleanthes held one virtue with many aspects, as follows from a soul that is wholly reason: for Plato many virtues follow from many parts of the soul, for the Stoics virtue and vice are states of the one reason, the same in gods and men, men and women. Ariston used this unity to deny that detailed precepts are of any use. Chrysippus's reconciliation was that the virtues are sciences, each with its own domain, coordinate rather than subordinate to a master virtue; they are qualities, breaths extending from the ruling part, and he wrote a book against Ariston to prove it. Like bodies in total mixture, the virtues are distinct yet interpenetrate: one cannot act by one without acting by all, as divine power is wholly present in each god. For the educator this means the soul can be reached by many equally fruitful routes, and dialectic and physics count as virtues too.

Bréhier finds in this a return to Socrates with a difference: each virtue is both concept and rule, knowledge and decision, so that the intellectualist theory absorbs the voluntarist one. Against Ariston's single block of virtue, Chrysippus asserts the competence of the sage: wisdom is not a sum of techniques, but it is their common foundation, the greatest and most varied power of action.

## The passions: the judgment that agitation is fitting (pp. 248–256)

Chrysippus distinguished physical pain, a painful sensation, from distress, the rational being's reaction to it. The sage's freedom from passion (*apatheia*) removes the distress, not the sensation. A passion is not an involuntary impression but a reaction that shakes the soul excessively, an impulse involving assent, and in human beings impulse always depends on judgment. For Plato and the Peripatetics, it is the virtuous act that is fixed by judgment, and faults arise from forces foreign to it, desire and anger; if the passions belong to a separate part of the soul, they can be natural when moderate. Bréhier argues that the ancients found the Stoic thesis hard because they confused the faculty of judging with judging correctly, a confusion that also clouds the Stoic theory.

A passion is "irrational" in two senses: it is contrary to reason to believe it fitting for the soul to be shaken by supposed goods or evils; and the movement is excessive, like a runner who cannot stop. Bréhier stresses that in grief the operative judgment is not "my friend's death is an evil" but "it is fitting to grieve over it"; Cicero reports that grief depends on believing it a just and due duty. He analyses a passion into weakness of soul, an opinion of good or evil, a judgment that the agitation is fitting, the excessive impulse, and its outward expression.

Galen reports that Zeno and Cleanthes placed the passion in the disturbance accompanying the judgment, Chrysippus in the judgment itself. The usual reading, Zeller's, takes Chrysippus to identify the passion with the opinion of good or evil. Bréhier argues this is wrong: the passion-judgment is the judgment of fittingness, which Chrysippus inserted between the value opinion and the disturbance. His reasons: no text identifies Galen's judgment with the value opinion; Zeno already defined distress as a fresh opinion of present evil, so the real difference must lie elsewhere; Chrysippus says angry people act against their own opinions, which makes sense only if the passion is the judgment that anger is fitting; Plutarch reports that he placed the passion not in any judgment but in the one that produces a violent impulse; and, decisively, Chrysippus distinguished the passion-judgment from error. The value opinion is an error, assent to a non-comprehensive impression; the passion is opinion in the sense of a weak supposition, showing not ignorance but the soul's weakness and inability to resist. Bréhier calls this Chrysippus's intellectualism in its most profound form: it puts weakness and failure of resistance into the judgment itself.

## The passions: sickness of the soul, time, and the remedies (pp. 256–262)

A second difference concerns what comes first. Cleanthes, true to his ethics of tension, rooted passion in weakness of soul, with false judgment as its result. For Chrysippus the essential thing is weakness of judgment, of which weakness of soul is one aspect; Bréhier takes Galen's efforts to make him admit a separate principle as evidence of the opposite. Chrysippus did recognise diseases of the soul, states in which reason has lost its direction, but as effects rather than causes: a passion is a fresh judgment prompted by the present impression, a movement, not a state; when the opinion hardens into habit, lasting sickness results. A passion is neither a disease nor a vice nor part of one.

The objection from time was that grief fades while the opinion that something evil has happened stays the same, so the passion must be something other than the opinion. Chrysippus answered that the opinion persists and what slackens is the contraction of the soul and the impulse toward it. Galen read this as admitting a cause other than judgment. Bréhier argues not: for a Stoic the rational impulse always includes a judgment or decision, and Plutarch confirms that the increase and decrease occur not in the judgment containing the error but in the pangs and contractions. So Chrysippus gave the passion some play relative to the value judgment. Identify the passion with the false value opinion and the doctrine looks contradictory, which pushes one to posit an irrational cause, the road Posidonius took by returning to Plato. Chrysippus's purpose in defining passions as judgments was to show how culpable they are and how far they are in our power, and so to found a therapy: correct the judgment and the passion is uprooted.

Cicero complains that Chrysippus treated remedies briefly. Where Cleanthes consoled by teaching wisdom, showing that the sage does not count the loss an evil, Chrysippus took the standpoint of the ordinary person and required wisdom only of the physician of the passions. Consolation mainly shows that grief is not a duty. One should not teach Stoic doctrine at the height of passion, but start provisionally from the sufferer's own beliefs; one should not treat passions while they are fresh; love and violent anger resist even one's own conscience. He preferred preventive remedies, meditations and maxims that accustom us in advance to possible ills.

Bréhier calls the theory of the passions, despite all its defects, a centrepiece of Chrysippus's work. Passion comes from us as rational action does; there is no external irrational force for the true self to tame. The enemy is within and expresses what we are, so we must transform ourselves. With this, he writes, the inner morality of conscience is ready to enter the scene.

## Society: the altruistic inclination and cosmopolitanism (pp. 262–270)

The early Stoics were not political reformers, and Zeller was roughly right that Stoicism produced no statesman: Plutarch noted how much they wrote on politics and how little they did. They accepted the Cynic critique of cities and laws, and Chrysippus piled up examples of the diversity of customs, burial, incest, the eating of the dead, to show that civic laws are conventions added to nature. But they did not condemn political life. Human society, stripped of convention, is natural, proved by a natural inclination toward others that grows out of the family inclination from which the first societies came.

Bréhier argues that these ideas, commonplace to us, were profoundly new. Plato ignores any natural altruism: sociability is imposed from outside by the city, and family feeling is the chief obstacle to civic feeling. As the city declined, thinkers either made the social bond a pure convention, like Epicurus, or grounded it in individual nature, like the Stoics.

This universal naturalism favours no particular constitution and so accepts all. The sage takes part in politics and marries if nothing prevents; he may profit from kingship or life at court. Against Kaerst's claim of a Stoic preference for monarchy, Bréhier replies that the texts may be later and that the favoured kingship is an ideal one: rule answerable to no one can belong only to the sage. The Stoics were conservatives, not revolutionaries, and no Stoic imagined an ideal state, above all because the ideal city already exists: the cosmos, whose citizens are gods and men and whose law is Zeus.

Following Gomperz, Bréhier holds that the Stoics were the first to separate society from the state, though their political vocabulary hides it: the cosmic city is not a world empire but the relations between human beings independent of any political form. The Greek city was defined by excluding foreigners, and justice stopped at its walls; the Stoic city unites the human race with the divine under one law of rational beings. Plato's city rests on difference and inequality; the Stoic society rests on equality, and with it appears for the first time the idea of the moral person as a member of society who need sacrifice nothing of himself to be social. On slavery Chrysippus was conservative, yet he treated the slave as a permanent hired worker bound by an exchange of services, who can even benefit his master. But Bréhier cautions that a truly universal humanity came only with the Roman Middle Stoa of Panaetius; with Chrysippus the divine city remains the privilege of a few sages.

## Justice as the virtue of the sage (pp. 270–273)

The individualist ideal of wisdom and tranquillity was nearly universal among the schools. In Epicureanism, relations with others may be added to it, but only in the individual's interest. What sets Stoicism apart, Bréhier argues, is that just relations with others are inseparable from wisdom. Against Plato, Chrysippus held that justice is wholly directed to others: one cannot wrong oneself, since injustice requires a plurality of beings, though he conceded that in a sense the unjust person harms himself.

A chain argument joins justice to every virtue: every right action is lawful and just, temperate and patient acts are right actions, so they are just; the link runs from right action to right reason, law and justice. Justice is the moral act seen as obedience to right reason, which commands and forbids. The sage is just not only as subject to law but as its judge and maker: justice is the science that gives each his due, the virtue of an impartial judge above the parties, and legislation is part of it. Bréhier recalls the moral character of ancient law codes, but notes that since the sage does not actually rule a city, his legislation tends to become purely moral, not a Platonic right of the wise to govern.

Justice also includes piety toward the gods and beneficence, gratitude and kindness toward men; Chrysippus discussed when and from whom to accept a benefit. This too belongs only to sages, who benefit one another by their least acts even without knowing each other: if one sage wisely stretches out a finger, all sages everywhere profit. Bréhier calls this a mystical society, an invisible sympathy in which reason is a cosmic force and a common good; hence the community of goods among sages, and in Chrysippus's *Republic* the community of women and children. The bad are excluded, exiles without city or home who can neither give nor receive a benefit.

He concludes that early Stoicism started two separate lines of thought about society: a humanitarian line, visible in the Roman jurists, of a human right above all civil right; and a mystical line, frequent in Hellenistic thought, of the city of God, of the saints and the elect.

## Conclusion: culture, common notions and Chrysippus's legacy (pp. 275–280)

Bréhier argues that the Hellenistic systems are best understood through their concern with education rather than pure speculation. Young men would not have followed years of teaching to learn the loose string of odd doctrines the doxographies present; they expected general culture. Philosophy as the vehicle of culture stood to the special sciences as the complete to the incomplete, and culture should not aim at originality: it expresses the most common and important ideas of a civilisation.

The Stoic idea of culture had three steps. It was in one sense a regression, taking back Presocratic physics and popular, scientifically crude notions such as the vital breath, fate, divination and astrology, because it meant to exclude no idea that counted in Greek civilisation, old or new. The theory of common notions supplied a first order: spontaneous knowledge is the base on which philosophical knowledge is built, as every virtuous act starts from a natural inclination, so culture is nature developed. Against the Cynics, who held civilisation and nature incompatible, the Stoics wanted agreement with both. Finally, the whole rests on a postulate: that all spontaneous knowledge and action agree at bottom, and science and virtue consist in becoming aware of that agreement. Bréhier raises an objection himself: an inclination counts as natural only if it fits the agreement that makes up virtue, so that pleasure and pity are called unnatural precisely because they disturb it, which looks circular. The Stoics' answer was a conviction that they would never have to choose between breaking up wisdom and mutilating nature, because nature is at bottom wisdom and providence. Their rule was to sacrifice nothing: not civilisation to nature, common sense to science, or political life to philosophy.

Chrysippus met the dangers Stoicism faced when he became its head. He cut it off from politics and drew it back into the school, preserving its dignity and a universal moral influence. Against the Academics he built a dialectic that did not discover doctrines but set them out more solidly. In physics, through total mixture, Zeno's system reached in him its fullest coherence. In ethics he reacted against a voluntarism that put all virtue into effort, holding that only knowledge can fix the highest end. The gravest crisis came after him, with Carneades, and the Stoicism of Panaetius and Posidonius abandoned his views on many points. But he fixed a lasting feature of the school: a rationalism that does not end in destructive criticism but gives fuller meaning to the concepts of physics, ethics and religion.
